Proposition three is about whether existing higher income-tax rates should expire. Voters approved these rates in twenty twelve and extended them in twenty sixteen. They are now scheduled to end after twenty thirty. The additional rates apply to roughly the highest-earning two percent of taxpayers. This is a tax on income, such as wages or investment profits, rather than a tax on everything someone owns. Proposition three would make those higher rates permanent. It would keep the current system beyond its scheduled expiration, rather than introducing a new increase in everybody’s rate today.

Imagine a teacher planning future classes and support services. If an existing revenue stream ends, a budget planner must consider cuts, replacement taxes, or other funding. Keeping that revenue can avoid that particular gap, but does not promise any school a specific staffing level. School budgets depend on the wider state budget.

The analyst estimates five to fifteen billion dollars of annual revenue maintained after the scheduled expiration. The wide range reflects the volatile incomes and investment gains of high earners. It is not a guaranteed fifteen billion every year. Roughly forty percent would support schools and community colleges. The remainder supports other state programs and reserves. Those shares describe the analyst’s estimate, rather than a promise that every dollar goes to classroom instruction. A no vote does not lower rates immediately. The scheduled expiration would still occur in twenty thirty-one. Comparing yes and no therefore means comparing future budgets, not just this year’s tax bill.

A voter might prefer protecting existing revenue for education and other services, with the additional tax concentrated on high earners. Making the rates permanent removes repeated expiration deadlines, though it does not make the revenue itself predictable.

You might ask whether a temporary tax should stay permanent. A skeptical voter could want the state to honor the sunset or reduce reliance on a small, volatile tax base. They may worry about effects on investment or where high earners live. The size of behavioral effects is uncertain.

In the September PPIC survey of likely voters, 58 percent said yes, 40 percent no, and 2 percent were unsure. These are dated survey results, not votes already cast. A later September Berkeley survey found 54 percent yes, 34 percent no, and 13 percent undecided. Question wording and methods differ, so comparing polls does not by itself establish a trend.
