A recall lets voters remove an elected official before the term ends. Proposition five changes what happens next. It does not eliminate the power to recall state officials. Today, a state recall ballot normally asks two questions: whether to remove the official, and who should replace them. If removal wins, the replacement candidate with the most votes takes office. Proposition five removes the replacement question. The office would be filled through succession, appointment, or a separate election, depending on the office and when the recall happens.

Imagine opening a ballot during a recall election. In a hypothetical crowded replacement contest, one candidate could lead with thirty votes out of a hundred. Winning the most votes is different from winning a majority. That is one concern motivating the change. Election staff would explain a different replacement process.

If a legislator is recalled, a special election would generally fill the seat. Vacancies in most other state offices would be filled by gubernatorial appointment. So a separate replacement vote is not guaranteed for every office. For the governor, the lieutenant governor would take over. If the recall happens early enough, before the specified nomination deadline in the first two years, voters elect a new governor at a later statewide election. Otherwise, the lieutenant governor completes the term. Costs could rise if an additional special election is needed, or fall when replacement-candidate materials disappear. The analyst says the net effect is unknown, with possible costs or savings of millions per recall.

A voter might want removal considered on its own, rather than as a shortcut for a candidate with limited support. Familiar vacancy procedures could make succession more predictable. That does not mean every replacement would be elected in a new majority vote.

You might want to choose the replacement yourself, immediately. Another voter could object to giving appointments or automatic succession a larger role. They may prefer seeing the alternatives on the same ballot, and worry that a later election leaves them waiting. These are choices about accountability, not just administrative convenience.

In the September PPIC survey of likely voters, 35 percent said yes, 59 percent no, and 5 percent were unsure. These are dated survey results, not votes already cast.
