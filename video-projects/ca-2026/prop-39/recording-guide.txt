The big picture
Proposition thirty-nine would add identification requirements each time Californians vote. Citizenship is already required for state and federal elections. The question is how identity and eligibility are checked, not whether those elections should allow noncitizens to vote. Today, registration uses identifying information. Most in-person voters sign a roster; mail voters sign their ballot envelope, and election officials check that signature. The proposal would add government-identification checks before ballots are counted.

An illustrative example
Imagine a voter preparing a mail ballot at home. Under the proposal, that voter would select a government-issued identification in their registration, then write its last four digits on the ballot envelope. Officials would compare those digits with the registration record. An election worker helps explain the identification process. An in-person voter would instead present government-issued identification. The state must offer a free voter identification card on request. The Legislature would still have to clarify details, including which forms of identification qualify.

How it would work
Officials would make best efforts using government data to verify citizenship on voter rolls, publish annual verification percentages, and face state compliance audits every other year. Those checks are separate from presenting identification at the polling place. The analyst estimates annual state and local costs from tens of millions to the low hundreds of millions of dollars. Card demand, implementation rules, staffing, and technology choices affect the total. A free card still costs government money to provide. This is a proposed change, not instructions for casting your current ballot. For voting today, use the official election instructions and your county elections office.

Why someone might vote yes
A voter might favor a consistent identification check as another verification layer and a way to strengthen confidence. The free-card provision addresses the purchase cost of identification. Whether the additional checks deliver benefits proportionate to their costs is the question to weigh.

Why someone might vote no
You might ask what happens to eligible voters without documents. A skeptical voter could worry about administrative mistakes, obtaining documents, or navigating new rules. A free card does not remove every time or paperwork burden. Existing checks already operate, so opponents question the extra system’s necessity and its effect on access.

What polling indicates
In the September PPIC survey of likely voters, 43 percent said yes, 55 percent no, and 1 percent were unsure. These are dated survey results, not votes already cast. A later September Berkeley survey found 39 percent yes, 52 percent no, and 9 percent undecided. Question wording and methods differ, so comparing polls does not by itself establish a trend. The Independent Voter Project survey ending October second found 43 percent yes and 40 percent no. Its methods and wording differ again. The disagreement is a reason for caution, not a reason to declare a winner.
