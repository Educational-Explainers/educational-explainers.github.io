Proposition forty-three changes the vote threshold for certain local taxes. A special tax pays for a specified purpose, such as a local service. A general tax supports general government spending. This measure focuses on special taxes proposed by voters. Government-proposed local special taxes generally need two-thirds approval. Court decisions allow voter-proposed special taxes to pass by a simple majority. Proposition forty-three would require two-thirds for those voter proposals too.

Imagine residents discussing a proposal for local services. Suppose a hundred people vote on a citizen-proposed special tax, and sixty vote yes. That is enough for a simple majority. It is short of two-thirds, so the same proposal would fail under the new requirement. Local services and the tax burden are both at stake.

Beginning January first, twenty twenty-seven, the higher threshold would apply to new, increased, or extended local special taxes proposed by voters. An extension matters because some existing taxes expire unless voters renew them. The proposition does not set a new tax rate or immediately cancel every existing tax. It changes the hurdle future proposals must clear. Voters would still decide whether a particular tax deserves support. The analyst expects local revenue could be lower than it otherwise would be, but gives no fixed amount. The effect depends on which future measures receive majority support but fall short of two-thirds.

A voter might want broad agreement before a dedicated local tax takes effect, regardless of whether a council or a citizen group proposes it. They may see one consistent threshold as protection for taxpayers who would have to pay despite voting no.

You might ask how much power a minority should have. Another voter could object that more than half the community may support a service but still be unable to fund it. In the example, forty no votes block sixty yes votes. A higher hurdle can protect dissenters while making collective action harder.

In the September PPIC survey of likely voters, 43 percent said yes, 51 percent no, and 6 percent were unsure. These are dated survey results, not votes already cast. A later September Berkeley survey found 31 percent yes, 40 percent no, and 29 percent undecided. Question wording and methods differ, so comparing polls does not by itself establish a trend.
