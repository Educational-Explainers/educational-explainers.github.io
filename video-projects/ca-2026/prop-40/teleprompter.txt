Proposition forty proposes a one-time tax on very large fortunes. Wealth means assets minus debts; income means earnings over a period. A person can own valuable company shares without receiving that value as cash income that year. The proposal reaches up to five percent of covered net worth for certain billionaires tied to California residency on January first, twenty twenty-six. It is not simply five percent of the amount above one billion dollars. Real estate and some retirement assets are generally excluded. Payment is due in twenty twenty-seven, with a more expensive option to spread payments across five years. The detailed valuation and threshold rules matter.

That is an illustration, not the calculation for every billionaire. A budget planner considers how long new money lasts. Ninety percent of revenue is directed to health care; the remainder supports education, food assistance, and administration. A one-time collection spread over years still does not become a permanent annual revenue stream.

The analyst expects temporary revenue totaling tens of billions, but the amount is hard to predict. Asset values, taxpayer responses, collection, and legal challenges create uncertainty. Administration could cost tens of millions annually for several years. There could also be an ongoing income-tax loss if wealthy residents change their behavior or leave. The analyst estimates that possible loss at less than one billion dollars per year. It is a different effect from the one-time wealth-tax revenue. A voter might see concentrated wealth as a source of funding for health care and other needs without broadly raising taxes. They may judge a temporary infusion worthwhile, even though it cannot solve every long-term funding problem.

You might question the economic and legal consequences. Another voter could object to taxing assets that are hard to sell or value, the residency date, or risks to investment and future tax receipts. They may prefer recurring funding with fewer collection uncertainties. These concerns do not establish that every dollar of projected revenue disappears.
