# Fiscal fact check: California November 3, 2026 Propositions 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 37, and 38

Scope is the fiscal claims on `ca-2026-ballot/index.html` (research snapshot dated for October 5, 2026). Primary sources are the Legislative Analyst’s Office ballot analyses, the Secretary of State official title and summary (Attorney General), the text of the proposed laws, and the LAO’s August 26, 2026 overview of the enacted 2026–27 spending plan. Campaign sites were not used as proof of dollar estimates. “The LAO said this” is separated from page inferences. Verdicts: ACCURATE, MOSTLY ACCURATE, INACCURATE, MISLEADING, or UNVERIFIED.

Page baseline, stated in the page itself: annual cost is compared with a **$251.477 billion enacted 2026–27 General Fund**. That figure is General Fund **expenditures**, not revenues. LAO, *The 2026–27 Budget: Overview of the Spending Plan* (August 26, 2026), Figure 3, “2026–27 Enacted” expenditures: **$251,477 million**. Same report, Figure 5 narrative: “General Fund spending is projected to total $252 billion in 2026–27.” Revenues and transfers in that same Figure 3 are **$226,754 million**. Ballot analyses round spending to “about $250 billion.” — [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)

Checked arithmetic against $251.477 billion (page’s baseline):

- $500 million / $251.477 billion = **0.1988 percent**, which rounds to **0.20 percent**.
- $600 million / $251.477 billion = **0.2386 percent**, which rounds to **0.24 percent**.
- $5 billion / $251.477 billion = **1.99 percent**, about **2.0 percent**.
- $15 billion / $251.477 billion = **5.96 percent**, about **6.0 percent**.

The LAO did **not** publish 0.20–0.24 percent or 2–6 percent. For Props 1 and 38 it published “about one-quarter of 1 percent (0.25 percent)” of the state’s total General Fund budget. Against the LAO’s own “about $250 billion” spending round number, $500–$600 million is 0.20–0.24 percent, so 0.25 percent is a coarser “about” rounding, not a different debt-service estimate. Using revenues ($226.754 billion) instead of spending would make $500–$600 million about **0.22–0.26 percent**. The page’s choice of the expenditure total matches how the LAO describes the budget (“plans to spend about $250 billion”).

## Proposition 1 — $11.25 billion housing bond

### Takeaway
The $11.25 billion authorization, the $10 billion / $1.25 billion split, and “$500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years” match the LAO exactly. Both pieces are general-obligation bonds, not a housing GO bond plus a CalVet revenue bond. Calling the whole $11.25 billion a general-obligation bond is accurate; the flow label “CalVet revenue bond” is not. “Tens of thousands” of units is the LAO’s own estimate (up to 40,000 rental units and up to 40,000 homeownership households), not only a campaign line. The 0.20–0.24 percent figure is the page’s arithmetic, not the LAO’s published “about 0.25 percent.”

### Cited Findings
- **Authorization, $11.25 billion. Verdict: ACCURATE.** LAO: “Proposition 1 allows the state to sell $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds for housing purposes. This amount consists of (1) a $10 billion bond for a variety of state housing programs that would be repaid by the General Fund and (2) a $1.25 billion bond that would be repaid by veterans’ payments on their home loans.” Attorney General: “Authorizes $11.25 billion in state general obligation bonds for housing affordability programs, including for California veterans.” — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/title-summary.htm)
- **Annual General Fund cost, “$500–600M a year for about 25 years.” Verdict: ACCURATE**, and it applies to the **$10 billion** housing bond, not to the veterans’ piece. LAO: “The state General Fund cost to repay the bond would be **$500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years**.” Fiscal-impact summary: “Increased state cost of $500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years to repay the housing bond.” The page’s cost sentence also says “CalVet loans are borrower-repaid,” which keeps the $11.25 billion headline from being read as all General Fund debt service. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)
- **LAO percentage vs page percentage. Verdict on “about 0.20–0.24% of the General Fund”: MOSTLY ACCURATE as page math; it is not an LAO figure.** LAO: “The estimated annual cost would be about one-quarter of 1 percent (0.25 percent) of the state’s total General Fund budget.” The page computes 0.20–0.24 percent from $500–$600 million and its $251.477 billion expenditure baseline (arithmetic above checks). Same 0.20–0.24 percent falls out of the LAO’s “about $250 billion” spending sentence. The LAO’s parenthetical 0.25 percent is a rounder “about” statement, not a higher debt-service range. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)
- **Annual-impact bar, low 0.5 / high 0.6 labeled “$500–600M / year.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** The page’s chart unit is billions (the General Fund bar is 251.477), so 0.5 and 0.6 are $500 million and $600 million. That matches the LAO range. It does not chart the 0.20–0.24 percent. — page `ca-2026-ballot/index.html`; [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)
- **Flow: housing bond $10B; housing programs $10B; veteran home loans $1.25B; General Fund $500–600M/yr for ~25 years; veteran payments repay bondholders. Verdict: ACCURATE on the dollars.** Those are the LAO’s two pieces and the LAO’s repayment paths. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)
- **Flow label “CalVet revenue bond $1.25B.” Verdict: INACCURATE.** The LAO calls the entire $11.25 billion “general obligation bonds,” and the veterans’ background is also general obligation: “the state sells general obligation bonds to investors” and “Participating veterans repay the cost of the bonds through their payments on their home loan.” The proposed law, Military and Veterans Code section 998.754(a), says the $1,250,000,000 of veterans’ bonds “shall be and constitute a valid and legally binding general obligation of the State of California, and the full faith and credit of the State of California is hereby pledged.” Section 998.754(b) then requires annual collection of state revenue to pay that debt service. The words “revenue bond” do not appear in the Prop 1 text. Statute: [SOS proposed-law PDF](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop1-text-proposed-laws.pdf). — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)
- **“Uses an established bond model; veteran loans are expected to repay themselves.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE.** LAO documents prior housing and veterans’ GO bonds (2024 housing bond of $2 billion; 2018 veterans’ bond of $1 billion) and states: “These payments have always covered the amount owed on the bonds, meaning the program has always operated at no direct cost to the state.” “Expected” is weaker than that historical record, and it is not a legal release of the full-faith-and-credit pledge. The payments reimburse a GO bond; they do not convert it into a revenue bond. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)
- **Big-number card: “Prop 1 · $11.25B · General-obligation bond. Housing authorization; General Fund repays most debt service.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** The LAO and the Attorney General call the whole authorization general-obligation bonds. “Most” is the right qualifier: the LAO assigns ongoing General Fund debt service to the $10 billion piece and no direct state cost to the $1.25 billion piece. A coarser voter-guide bond box lists “Authorized new borrowing $11.25 billion,” “Annual cost to pay off bond $500 million to $600 million,” “Likely repayment period 25 years,” and “Source of repayment: General tax revenue,” which flattens the veterans’ repayment path the analysis body spells out. The page’s “most” is closer to the analysis body than that box is. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [voter-guide analysis PDF snippet](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop1-title-summ-analysis.pdf)
- **“Funds tens of thousands of affordable units and homeownership loans.” Verdict: ACCURATE as a paraphrase of the LAO, not a campaign-only number.** LAO “Impact of Funds”: “The bond funds would provide subsidies for up to 40,000 multifamily rental units, as well as about 2,500 units for farmworkers and about 1,200 beds for university students. The funds also would help up to 40,000 households, including about 2,100 veterans, with homeownership by providing homebuyer assistance and supporting the production and repair of homes.” The official pro argument on the Secretary of State quick guide does **not** use “tens of thousands”; the unit counts are in the LAO analysis. “Up to” is not a guarantee, and the 40,000 households are not an additional 40,000 newly built units. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [SOS quick guide pro argument](http://quickguidetoprops.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2026-11-03/1)
- **Program split inside the $10 billion, which the page’s flow collapses to “$10B total.”** LAO: affordable multifamily **$7.2 billion**; homeownership **$1.1 billion**; infrastructure **$500 million**; farmworker **$450 million**; UC/CSU student housing **$350 million** (split evenly); tribal **$200 million**; local pilots **$200 million**. Those sum to $10 billion. Attorney General rollup is the same money in coarser lines: $5.1 billion multifamily construction/rehab; $2.1 billion acquire/rehab/preserve and supportive housing ($5.1 + $2.1 = $7.2); $1.25 billion veterans; $1.1 billion homeownership; $1 billion farmworker, tribal, and student ($450 + $200 + $350 million); $500 million infrastructure; $200 million local programs. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/title-summary.htm)
- **Omitted LAO total-cost fact.** “Since the state has to pay interest on the money it borrows, the total cost of the bond would be about 15 percent more (after adjusting for inflation) than if the state paid up front with money it already has.” That 15 percent is a real, inflation-adjusted premium on the housing bond. It is not an extra annual cost on top of the $500–$600 million; the annual figure already is principal plus interest. Administrative costs are covered by “a portion of the bond funds,” not a separate General Fund line. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026)

### Inferences
- Legally, full faith and credit covers the veterans’ $1.25 billion as well as the $10 billion. The LAO’s “no direct state cost” is the historical and expected fiscal result (veteran payments have always covered debt service), not a statement that the General Fund has no contingent pledge. The page’s big-number card gets the practical split right; the flow’s “revenue bond” label gets the legal form wrong.
- A reader who stops at “$11.25B” and “$500–600M a year” can still tell that most, not all, of the authorization is General Fund debt service, because the cost sentence says CalVet loans are borrower-repaid. The material miss is the bond type, not the annual dollar range.

### Gaps
- The voter-guide bond-box line “Source of repayment: General tax revenue” was taken from the official analysis PDF text returned in search, not from a full re-extraction of that PDF’s graphic. The LAO narrative, which distinguishes the two repayment sources, is the clearer primary source and was read in full.

## Proposition 2 — rainy-day cap and extra debt payments

### Takeaway
Raising the cap from 10 percent to 20 percent and extending the **requirement** for extra debt payments from 2030 through 2040 matches the LAO. There is no fixed dollar estimate. “About $20 billion” of broadly usable reserves is the LAO’s figure for reserves that can be used for any program, equal to the enacted plan’s Budget Stabilization Account plus the Special Fund for Economic Uncertainties at the end of 2026–27 ($19.6 billion), not the school reserve and not the Governor’s larger “nearly $30 billion” talking point. The rebate sentence is an inference, not an LAO ballot finding.

### Cited Findings
- **“Raises the reserve ceiling and extends required extra debt payments through 2040.” Verdict: ACCURATE**, with an omitted expansion of what those payments may buy. LAO proposal: deposits continue “until it reaches 20 percent of General Fund taxes (instead of 10 percent of General Fund taxes).” “Proposition 2 also requires the state to make extra debt payments through 2040 (instead of through 2030).” LAO spending-plan summary of ACA 20: the measure “would raise the cap on the BSA’s balance from 10 percent to 20 percent of General Fund tax revenues” and “extend the state’s current requirement to make extra debt payments toward pension and retiree health liabilities—set to expire in 2030—through 2040, while expanding the purposes of those payments.” — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)
- **Base of the percentage.** Ballot LAO says “General Fund taxes.” The spending plan says “General Fund tax revenues.” The Attorney General says “Doubles the budget reserve cap from 10% to 20% of General Fund revenues, requiring savings until Fund’s balance equals 20% of General Fund.” The page flow says only “Ceiling rises from 10% to 20%” and “10% ceiling remains,” without naming the base. The 10-to-20 change is accurate; the omitted base is General Fund taxes / tax revenues in the LAO’s fiscal writing, which is narrower than all General Fund revenues. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/title-summary.htm)
- **Fail flow: “10% ceiling remains” and “Requirement ends in 2030.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** LAO background: “Once money in the rainy day fund reaches 10 percent of General Fund taxes, the state is no longer required to make additional deposits.” “The requirement for extra debt payments ends in 2030.” “Extra Debt Payments Optional After 2030. … After that, the Legislature and the Governor could decide to deposit the money for extra debt payments into the rainy day fund instead. Or … could make smaller extra debt payments.” The page says the **requirement** ends, which matches. It would be too strong to say the payments themselves must stop in 2030. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026)
- **“Structural change; no fixed dollar estimate.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** LAO fiscal summary and ballot label: “State budget reserves would be higher.” No dollar estimate of added reserves or added debt payments. Related qualitative effects the page does not put in the cost line: “Possibly More Debt Payments”; larger deposits “in years when revenues from taxes on investment gains are very high.” — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026)
- **“Current broadly usable reserves are about $20B.” Verdict: ACCURATE as the LAO’s phrase, with the account and date specified here.** Ballot LAO: “Currently, the state has about $20 billion in reserves that can be used for any public program.” Spending plan, August 26, 2026, reflecting administration estimates of actions through July 1, 2026, Figure 3, **2026–27 enacted year-end**: BSA **$15,070 million**, SFEU **$4,500 million**, total reserves **$19,570 million**. Narrative: “Combined, under the spending plan, the BSA ($15.1 billion) and SFEU ($4.5 billion) would total $19.6 billion at the end of 2026–27.” “Nearly $20 billion” and “about $20 billion” are that sum. The Safety Net Reserve “is now fully depleted.” Separately, “the state would also end 2026–27 with $9.2 billion in the Public School System Stabilization Account, or Proposition 98 Reserve,” which is **not** usable for any program. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)
- Same Figure 3 shows a much higher **2025–26 revised** total of **$40,719 million** (BSA $11,496 million + SFEU $29,223 million). The “about $20 billion” line is the enacted 2026–27 year-end projection after that drawdown, not cash on an earlier date and not $40.7 billion. The Governor’s June 29, 2026 signing statement said the budget “preserves nearly $30 billion in reserves — and just over $35 billion when additional holding accounts are included.” That is a different, larger definition. The page tracks the LAO, not the Governor’s press total. — [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197); [Governor’s office, June 29, 2026](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/06/29/signedbudget/)
- **What the extended debt payments may be used for, which the page’s one-line does not say.** Today they may be used only for “long-term pension and retiree health care debts.” Under Prop 2 they could also “be used to make required payments to schools and community colleges, repay borrowing from other state funds, and repay certain loans from the federal government.” The amount remains tied to the basic rainy-day deposit. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026)
- **“Excluding deposits from the spending limit can make taxpayer rebates less likely.” Verdict: not an LAO finding. It is a page con argument. As a directional inference it is MOSTLY ACCURATE; it is not quantified, and it is not the near-term budget situation.** Ballot LAO: “Deposits into the rainy day fund would not count toward the state appropriations limit. Instead, the money would count toward the limit when it is taken out.” A second surge account (the spending plan identifies the Temporary Surplus Holding Account) gets the same treatment, “could not be larger than 10 percent of General Fund taxes in each year.” The ballot analysis never says “rebate.” The spending plan, discussing the limit generally: in 2026–27 the state has “$27 billion in room” ($27.3 billion in Figure 7), “the state does not have excess revenues in 2026–27, meaning no additional funding for schools or taxpayer rebates is triggered under Article XIII B,” and Prop 2 “would change how both BSA and TSHA deposits count toward the SAL” beginning in 2027–28. Excluding deposits can make it harder to hit the limit, and hitting the limit is what triggers rebates. The LAO does not say rebates become less likely, and the current $27.3 billion of room means no rebate is triggered this year either way. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)

### Inferences
- “Broadly usable” on the page is a fair gloss of the LAO’s “can be used for any public program,” i.e. BSA + SFEU, excluding the $9.2 billion school reserve. Adding the school reserve would produce a figure closer to the high $20 billions and would misread the LAO sentence.
- The Attorney General’s short summary omits the 2040 debt-payment extension and the spending-limit change. The page, following the LAO analysis rather than the short AG bullets, is the more complete fiscal description on those two points.

### Gaps
- No LAO table was found that breaks the ballot analysis’s “about $20 billion” into BSA versus SFEU inside the voter guide itself. The $15.1 billion / $4.5 billion split is from the August 26, 2026 spending plan’s enacted-budget estimates, which is the best dated official reconciliation.
- The proposed constitutional text’s exact SAL wording was seen in the voter-guide text PDF (Projected Surplus Temporary Holding Account transfers excluded up to 10 percent of General Fund proceeds of taxes). A full side-by-side of every BSA formula change in ACA 20 was not re-derived beyond the LAO’s description.

## Proposition 3 — extend Proposition 30 / 55 high-income rates

### Takeaway
The rates are the Proposition 30 (2012) add-on brackets extended by Proposition 55 (2016). They already apply; Prop 3 would stop them from expiring. The LAO’s revenue range is exactly “$5 billion to $15 billion” a year, varying with the stock market, maintained instead of lost in 2031. “About 2–6 percent” is the page’s arithmetic, not an LAO percentage. The 40/60 flow correctly cites the LAO and should not be “corrected” to the Attorney General’s 89/11 line, which is the split **inside** the education dollars. Health care has a real but capped Medi-Cal set-aside (50 percent of the remainder, maximum $2 billion). Reserves are not a dedicated percentage.

### Cited Findings
- **“Keeps the current top income-tax rates after their scheduled 2031 expiration.” Verdict: ACCURATE** against the LAO and the Attorney General. LAO: “Proposition 3 makes the higher income tax rates established by Proposition 30 permanent instead of letting them expire in 2031.” “Voters approved Proposition 30 in 2012 to temporarily increase income tax rates on high-income taxpayers. In 2016, voters approved Proposition 55 to extend these higher rates until 2030.” Attorney General: “Makes permanent the existing 2012 voter-approved tax rates for high-income Californians, currently set to expire in 2031.” The measure’s own findings say “Unless the tax is extended by the voters, it will expire in 2030.” That is the last tax year the higher rates apply (Proposition 55’s “until 2030”). The operative sunset printed in the constitutional text is “This paragraph shall become inoperative on December 1, 2031.” The page’s 2031 matches the LAO and the official title, not the findings’ “2030.” — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/title-summary.htm); [text of proposed law](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop3-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **Who pays. “Existing higher rates on roughly the top 2% of earners.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE.** LAO, without “roughly” and without “earners”: “The top 2 percent of California taxpayers pay these higher rates. These taxpayers pay about half of all state income taxes.” The measure’s findings also say “the top 2%.” Attorney General dollar thresholds, which the page does not quote: “Rates apply to income over about $371,000 for single filers, $742,000 for joint filers, and $505,000 for heads of household (2025 levels; adjusted annually for inflation).” The findings use rounder statutory floors: the measure “does not increase taxes on couples earning less than $680,000 or individuals earning less than $340,000, in today’s dollars.” The constitutional brackets printed in the text are 10.3 percent, 11.3 percent, and 12.3 percent above those floors (the Proposition 30 increments). — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/title-summary.htm); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop3-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **“Those higher rates expire in 2031; no one’s rate changes today.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** Same LAO and AG expiration language. Findings: “It does not change the existing tax rates, which have remained the same since this tax measure was first enacted in 2012.” The LAO yes/no statement: an increase “in place since 2012 would become permanent instead of expiring in 2031.” The page fail-flow note “No rate changes before 2031 under either outcome” matches that. — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop3-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **“Maintains $5–15B in annual revenue starting in 2031.” Verdict: ACCURATE on the dollars and the start year.** LAO: “The amount of money brought in by Proposition 3 will go up and down a lot from year to year. … In a weak year, the proposition might bring in around $5 billion in revenue to the state. In a strong year, the proposition might bring in $15 billion in revenue to the state. In most years, revenue would be in between these amounts.” Summary: “Maintains $5 billion to $15 billion of annual state income tax revenue by making a temporary tax increase on high-income earners permanent instead of letting it expire in 2031.” The range is weak-year versus strong-year, not a steady band. “Starting in 2031” is when the revenue would otherwise disappear; nothing in the LAO says rates or revenue change before then. The measure’s findings claim “an immediate reduction to state revenues of over $10 billion,” which is inside the LAO range but is not the LAO’s estimate. The page uses the LAO range, not the findings’ “over $10 billion.” — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop3-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **“About 2–6% of today’s General Fund.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE as page arithmetic; the LAO did not publish the percentage.** $5 billion and $15 billion are about 2 percent and 6 percent of both the page’s $251.477 billion and the LAO’s “about $250 billion” of General Fund spending. See arithmetic at the top. — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)
- **Flow: “About 40%” schools and colleges, “About 60%” other programs and reserves. “Widths use LAO’s approximate 40/60 distribution.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE, and correctly attributed to the LAO.** LAO: “Roughly 40 percent of the funding would go to schools and community colleges. The rest would go to other state programs. Part of this amount would be set aside in budget reserves the state could use to fund these programs when revenues decline.” “Schools & colleges” is slightly loose: the 40 percent is schools and **community colleges**, not the University of California or California State University. “Other programs & reserves” matches “the rest,” but reserves are only “part” of that rest, not a guaranteed 60 percent deposit. The 40 percent is the LAO’s estimate of the Proposition 98 share of this revenue. It is not a statutory 40 percent rate. — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026)
- **Do not replace 40/60 with the Attorney General’s 89/11.** AG bullet: “Allocates tax revenues 89% to K–12 schools, 11% to community colleges.” In the proposed law that 89/11 is the split of “the moneys appropriated for education,” not of the entire tax: “Eleven percent of the moneys appropriated for education” to community colleges and “Eighty-nine percent of the moneys appropriated for education” to schools, county offices, and charter schools. That is the existing Education Protection Account split continued from Proposition 30/55. ### Inferences
- A reader of the flow will think about 40 percent of a $5–$15 billion stream goes to K–14 education and about 60 percent is ordinary General Fund money, some of which existing law may save. The LAO’s “higher income tax rates” are the whole Proposition 30 high-income structure (the three add-on brackets in the text: 10.3, 11.3, and 12.3 percent). The page’s “top rates” is a slight narrowing of a multi-bracket structure.

### Gaps
- Strikeout versus italic in the voter-guide text PDF is not preserved by text extraction, so which printed sentences are deletions versus new law was resolved by the LAO and the Attorney General (the measure makes the temporary rates permanent) rather than by font styling.
- The LAO’s Figure 1 rate table is an image. The bracket rates and floors above are from the proposed constitutional text and the AG summary, not from a transcription of that figure. The separate 1 percent Mental Health Services Tax was not verified as part of Prop 3 and is not included here.

### Takeaway

## Proposition 5 — recall replacement question

### Takeaway
The LAO’s fiscal line is “net fiscal effect unknown,” with possible savings or costs “of millions of dollars,” and those effects “would be rare” and “less than” 0.1 percent of the General Fund. The page’s “well below 0.1 percent” is a gloss. The official title says “Statewide Officers,” but the LAO and the constitutional text remove the same-day replacement vote for state officers generally, including legislators. That scope difference matters because the LAO’s example of new costs is a legislative special election.

### Cited Findings
- **Which offices. Page: “Removes the same-day replacement vote from recalls of statewide officers.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE against the official title; incomplete against the LAO and the text.** Official title: “Changes Recall Election Process for Statewide Officers.” LAO proposal: “Proposition 5 removes the second question—who should replace a recalled official—from a recall election. … voters would only decide whether to remove an elected official from a state office.” “In the case of an office in the state Senate or Assembly, this means that a replacement typically would be elected through a separate special election. The Governor would choose who would fill a vacancy in any other office (other than office of the Governor).” AG: under current law voters decide whether “to recall a state officer” and who replaces that officer; under the measure, “for all other recalled officers, the office will remain vacant until filled by a subsequent special election or appointment as specified in existing law.” The text amends Article II, section 15, the general recall section (“whether to recall an officer”), and strikes the successor election so that “the office shall be vacant.” It is not limited to the eight statewide constitutional officers. — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/title-summary.htm); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop5-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **“A successful recall creates a vacancy filled by succession, appointment, or a later special election.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE.** LAO: the office becomes vacant. Governor: if recalled before the close of the nomination period for the next statewide election during the first two years of the term, the Lieutenant Governor serves “until voters elect a new Governor at a future statewide election”; if recalled later, the Lieutenant Governor serves “for the rest of the term.” The text is more specific than “a later special election”: that early-term gubernatorial election “shall be consolidated with the statewide primary election and, if necessary, the subsequent statewide general election.” Other executive offices: Governor appointment. Legislative seats: typically a special election. AG also: “a recalled officer may be a candidate in a special election to fill the office,” and the text bars appointing the recalled officer to the vacancy. — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026); [SOS title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/title-summary.htm); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop5-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **“Rare-event savings or costs in the millions; net effect unknown.” Verdict: ACCURATE.** LAO summary: “Net fiscal effect unknown. Possible state and local government savings or costs of millions of dollars to administer recall elections. These costs or savings would be rare, and depend on the office recalled and how often recalls occur.” Body: a standalone special election to fill a legislative seat “could be millions of dollars per election.” Savings from dropping the replacement question “could be millions of dollars per election.” Ballot label: “Net fiscal effect unknown. In the event of a recall, savings or costs of millions of dollars to administer the election depending on the office recalled.” — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026)
- **“Well below 0.1% of the budget.” Verdict: MOSTLY ACCURATE; “well below” is the page’s gloss.** LAO: “This net fiscal effect represents less than one-tenth of 1 percent (0.1 percent) of the state’s total General Fund budget.” The LAO says “less than,” not “well below,” and it does not publish a tighter percentage. How state and county governments share the cost “would depend on future decisions by the Legislature.” — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026)
- **Historical frequency, LAO background, not a future estimate:** “Since 1913, 11 state recall efforts have qualified for the ballot (nine legislators and two Governors) and six have resulted in an official’s removal from office (five legislators and one Governor).” The 2003 gubernatorial recall had 135 replacement candidates; 2021 had 46. — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026)

### Inferences
- The fiscal “millions” are per recall election, not an annual budget item. The page’s “rare-event” label matches the LAO. A reader who thinks only statewide officers are covered will miss the scenario the LAO uses for added cost: a legislative vacancy that needs its own special election.
- Judges are not given a separate LAO fiscal hypothetical. The amended sentence refers to offices filled under Article VI, section 16(d), which the existing constitution treats differently from a successor election. Whether every judicial office is inside the measure was not given its own LAO paragraph.

### Gaps
- No separate LAO dollar example was found for a statewide judicial recall. The “millions” quotes are the legislative special-election cost and the ballot-printing savings, not a judicial figure.

- **“Includes audits, an admin cap, revenue sharing, and a California price discount.” Verdict: ACCURATE that each feature exists. The page does not state the rates.**
  - Audits: LAO, the institute “must issue a public report on its activities and have an independent financial audit,” and the council has “public reporting and audit requirements.” The text requires grant recipients to submit to audits. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)
  - Admin cap: “No more than 2 percent of total bond funds may be used for state administrative costs” (LAO). Statute: “not to exceed 2 percent of all moneys disbursed” for department, council, committee, and Controller administrative costs. A separate grant rule limits indirect costs to 20 percent of a research grant unless the grantee matches above 20 percent. The page’s “an admin cap” fits the 2 percent state cap; the 20 percent indirect-cost cap is an additional limit. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop38-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
  - Revenue sharing: LAO, “Ten percent of any revenue generated must be given to the state. This revenue must first go into the state General Fund until it repays the cost of the research bond (the amount borrowed plus the interest). Any additional revenue would be used for future immunology and immunotherapy research.” Statute: recipients pay “10 percent of all revenues derived … from the sale, licensing, or any other commercialization or monetization” of intellectual property arising from the funded research. AG short form: “General Fund receives 10% of licensing royalties until bonds are repaid.” — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop38-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
  - Price discount: LAO, “Any treatment developed through funded research and sold in California must generally be offered at a 20 percent discount.” Statute: “at least 20 percent below the national average price, calculated by excluding California.” The page says “a California price discount,” not the 20 percent or the excluding-California formula. The feature exists. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop38-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- **Also in the LAO, not on the page’s feature list:** grant recipients “must buy at least half of their goods and services from California suppliers when reasonably possible.” The text phrases this as a good-faith effort to achieve “more than 50 percent” of purchases from California suppliers. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)
- **Omitted real interest premium.** LAO: total cost “would be about 10 percent more (after adjusting for inflation)” than paying up front. As with Prop 1, that is not stacked on top of the $500–$600 million annual figure. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)
- **Omitted near-term General Fund cash, structured as a loan.** Statute: “there is hereby appropriated from the General Fund, as a source of a start-up loan or loans to the institute, one hundred million dollars ($100,000,000).” The loan “shall be repaid to the General Fund within 12 months” of the related bond draw, “or if not repaid within 12 months, as soon thereafter as is practicable as determined by the Treasurer.” The LAO ballot analysis reviewed here does not mention this $100 million appropriation. It does not change the $500–$600 million annual debt-service estimate if it is repaid from bond proceeds as written. It is still a General Fund outlay at the start. — [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop38-text-proposed-laws.pdf); [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)
- **Indirect budget effects the page does not mention.** LAO: effects on state health programs such as Medi-Cal are uncertain, because new treatments “could either increase or decrease” the average cost of care. — [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)

### Inferences
- The flow’s 50/50 is the right picture of who receives the bond proceeds. The disease rule is a $2.1 billion floor inside each half, so a reader who thinks the entire institute award is unrestricted, or who thinks cancer/heart/Alzheimer’s research is a separate competing half, would be wrong. The page’s pro line listing those diseases “and other conditions” is consistent with a floor, not a ceiling.
- “One selected institute” plus the con that half the money goes there is what the statute does. The selection criteria are specific enough that “selected later” should not be pictured as an open statewide RFP among many similar institutes.

### Gaps
- Whether any existing institute uniquely satisfies the volume, square-footage, and philanthropy tests was not investigated. The text sets the tests; it does not print an institute’s name.
- The LAO says revenue goes to the General Fund “until it repays the cost of the research bond” and then to future research. The operative waterfall after the bond is repaid was not re-quoted section by section beyond the LAO and the 10 percent payment duty. The findings’ “fully recoup” sentence is not treated as the fiscal estimate.

## Cross-cutting omissions that change scale

### Takeaway
The page’s headline dollar ranges for Props 1, 3, 5, 37, and 38 match the LAO. The systematic differences are bond type on the CalVet slice, page-computed percentages versus the LAO’s “about 0.25 percent,” and a few allocation rules that change who is guaranteed money inside an otherwise correct total.

### Cited Findings
- Prop 1 flow calls the $1.25 billion veterans’ piece a revenue bond. Statute and LAO call it a general-obligation bond with full faith and credit, repaid in practice by veterans. That is the only clear **INACCURATE** bond-type label in this set. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop1-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Props 1 and 38 percentages (0.20–0.24 percent) and the Prop 3 percentage (2–6 percent) are page calculations on the enacted $251.477 billion General Fund expenditure total. The LAO published about 0.25 percent for the two bonds and did not publish a percentage for Prop 3. The underlying dollar ranges are the LAO’s. — [LAO Prop 1](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=1&year=2026); [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026); [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [LAO spending plan](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/5197)
- Prop 5 “well below 0.1 percent” softens the LAO’s “less than … 0.1 percent,” and “statewide officers” follows the title while the fiscal example in the LAO is a legislative special election. — [LAO Prop 5](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=5&year=2026)
- Prop 2’s rebate sentence is not in the ballot analysis. The spending-limit exclusion is. — [LAO Prop 2](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=2&year=2026)
- Prop 3’s 40/60 is the LAO estimate. The statutory Medi-Cal piece is capped at $2 billion. The 89/11 figures are the split of the education appropriation only. — [LAO Prop 3](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=3&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop3-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Prop 37’s “no direct cost” matches the LAO and is not contradicted by a hidden General Fund pledge. The bonds disclaim the state’s faith and credit. — [LAO Prop 37](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=37&year=2026); [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop37-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Prop 38’s 50/50 is statutory. At least $2.1 billion of each half is restricted to cancer, heart disease, and Alzheimer’s. A $100 million General Fund start-up loan is appropriated and is supposed to be repaid from bond proceeds. — [text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop38-text-proposed-laws.pdf); [LAO Prop 38](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=38&year=2026)

### Inferences
- None of the annual General Fund ranges on the page ($500–$600 million for Props 1 and 38, $5–$15 billion for Prop 3, a few hundred thousand dollars for Prop 4, millions per rare recall for Prop 5, and no direct cost for Prop 37) contradict the LAO’s published ranges. The corrections that would change a careful reader’s picture are the CalVet bond type, the difference between 0.20–0.24 percent and the LAO’s about 0.25 percent, Prop 5’s inclusion of legislators, Prop 3’s capped health formula versus an open-ended “health care and reserves” dedication, and Prop 38’s $4.2 billion disease floor inside the 50/50 split.

### Gaps
- No claim in this set was left without an LAO or statutory source except the page’s “replenish” wording on Prop 37 and the unquantified rebate inference on Prop 2, both flagged above.
