# California November 3, 2026 statewide ballot inventory and legal effect

Sources are California Secretary of State (SOS) pages and the Official Voter Information Guide (VIG) for the November 3, 2026 General Election, including Attorney General title and summary, “What Your Vote Means,” Legislative Analyst analysis, and the text of proposed laws, plus the California Constitution on leginfo. Pages were retrieved October 5, 2026. The VIG certificate is dated August 10, 2026. Proposition text PDFs were created August 26, 2026. The certification press release is dated June 25, 2026. Where the June 25 circulating summary and the later VIG title and summary differ, the VIG is treated as the ballot-pamphlet text.

Verdict scale used below: ACCURATE, MOSTLY ACCURATE, INACCURATE, MISLEADING, UNVERIFIED, SOURCE URL BROKEN.

## Is November 3, 2026 the California statewide general election, and are there exactly these 14 propositions?

### Takeaway
Yes. November 3, 2026 is the statewide General Election, and the qualified statewide ballot is exactly Propositions 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, and 45. Nothing numbered 6 through 36 is on that ballot. Two other measures are qualified, but for 2028, not for this election. ACA 13 was removed from this ballot on June 25, 2026.

## Proposition 1 — housing bond

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE to ACCURATE on the legal effect. The $11.25 billion figure and the yes/no bond choice match the VIG. The page’s short title is a paraphrase, not the official title, and the one-liner drops several statutory uses of the bond.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “AUTHORIZES BONDS FOR HOUSING AFFORDABILITY PROGRAMS. LEGISLATIVE STATUTE.” Attorney General summary: “Authorizes $11.25 billion in state general obligation bonds for housing affordability programs, including for California veterans, and to finance housing development projects seeking federal tax credits.” Allocations stated in the summary: “$5.1 billion” multifamily rental; “$2.1 billion” acquire, rehabilitate, or preserve affordable housing and supportive housing for people experiencing homelessness; “$1.25 billion” state-funded mortgages for California veterans; “$1.1 billion” homeownership and downpayment assistance; “$1 billion” farmworker, tribal, and student housing; “$500 million” housing infrastructure; “$200 million” local housing programs. Those seven amounts sum to $11.25 billion. — [Prop 1 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/title-summary.htm)
- “A YES vote on this measure means: The state could borrow $11.25 billion to support veterans and affordable housing.” “A NO vote on this measure means: The state could not borrow $11.25 billion to support veterans and affordable housing.” — [Prop 1](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/1/)
- Page short title “Housing affordability bond” is not the official title. One-liner “Borrow $11.25B for affordable housing, homeownership help, and veteran loans” matches the dollar amount and three of the uses, and omits farmworker, tribal, and student housing, housing infrastructure, local programs, preservation and homelessness supportive housing, and federal-tax-credit projects. YES “California may issue $11.25B in bonds for housing programs and CalVet loans” matches the official yes box’s $11.25 billion borrow authority; “CalVet” is a shorthand for the summary’s “state-funded mortgages for California veterans” ($1.25 billion), not the whole bond. NO “The state may not issue these bonds” matches the official no box.

### Inferences
- Dollar amount, general-obligation borrow authority, and the yes/no choice: ACCURATE.
- Short title and one-liner: MOSTLY ACCURATE. They are fair labels, not the official title, and they omit uses that are part of the $11.25 billion authorization.

### Gaps
- None on the $11.25 billion authorization itself.

## Proposition 2 — rainy-day fund

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. Raising the reserve cap from 10 percent to 20 percent and extending extra debt payments through 2040 are what the Legislative Analyst describes. The page omits other constitutional changes that are part of the same measure.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “INCREASES STATE’S RAINY DAY FUND. LEGISLATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT.” Attorney General summary: “Doubles the budget reserve cap from 10% to 20% of General Fund revenues, requiring savings until Fund’s balance equals 20% of General Fund.” Also: “Requires additional savings in years when tax revenues are high.” — [Prop 2 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “Rules for building state budget reserves and paying down extra state debts would change. As a result, state budget reserves would be higher. The state also would be required to make extra debt payments for longer.” Official no: “Rules for building state budget reserves and paying down extra state debts would not change.” — [Prop 2](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/)
- Legislative Analyst: “Proposition 2 requires the state to make deposits into the rainy day fund until it reaches 20 percent of General Fund taxes (instead of 10 percent of General Fund taxes).” “Proposition 2 also requires the state to make extra debt payments through 2040 (instead of through 2030).” The text changes the constitutional date from the “2030–31” fiscal year to the “2040–41” fiscal year and the cap from “10” to “20 percent.” The Analyst also says the extra payments “could be used to make required payments to schools and community colleges, repay borrowing from other state funds, and repay certain loans from the federal government,” and that deposits into the rainy day fund “would not count toward the state appropriations limit” until withdrawn. — [Prop 2 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2/analysis.htm); [Prop 2 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop2-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Page: “Raises the reserve ceiling and extends required extra debt payments through 2040.” YES: “Rules would build higher reserves and continue extra debt payments for longer.” NO: “Current reserve and debt-payment rules stay in place.”

### Inferences
- The two things the one-liner names, and the yes/no paraphrase of the vote-means box: ACCURATE.
- As a full account of the legal effect: MOSTLY ACCURATE. Omitted and material: expanded uses of the extra debt payments, and the new rule that rainy-day deposits (and certain other set-asides, capped at 10 percent of General Fund taxes) do not count toward the state appropriations limit until the money is taken out.

### Gaps
- None on the 10-to-20 percent cap or the 2040 date.

## Proposition 3 — high-income tax rates

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. The rates become permanent instead of expiring in 2031, and the Legislative Analyst says the top 2 percent of taxpayers pay them. The page’s “roughly” and “earners,” and its silence on the school-allocation rules, are the misses. A no vote does not change anyone’s rate today.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “PROVIDES PERMANENT FUNDING FOR SCHOOLS AND HEALTH CARE BY EXTENDING EXISTING TAX ON HIGH INCOMES. INITIATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT.” Attorney General: “Makes permanent the existing 2012 voter-approved tax rates for high-income Californians, currently set to expire in 2031.” “Rates apply to income over about $371,000 for single filers, $742,000 for joint filers, and $505,000 for heads of household (2025 levels; adjusted annually for inflation).” “Allocates tax revenues 89% to K–12 schools, 11% to community colleges.” “Allows local school boards to decide how revenues are spent; bars use for administrative costs.” — [Prop 3 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “An income tax increase on high-income earners in place since 2012 would become permanent instead of expiring in 2031.” Official no: “An income tax increase on high-income earners in place since 2012 would expire in 2031.” — [Prop 3](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/)
- Legislative Analyst: “Voters approved Proposition 30 in 2012 to temporarily increase income tax rates on high-income taxpayers. In 2016, voters approved Proposition 55 to extend these higher rates until 2030.” “The top 2 percent of California taxpayers pay these higher rates.” “Proposition 3 makes the higher income tax rates established by Proposition 30 permanent instead of letting them expire in 2031.” — [Prop 3 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/3/analysis.htm)
- The June 25, 2026 certification summary used 2024 thresholds (“about $360,000” single, “$721,000” joint, “$490,000” head of household). The VIG uses the 2025 figures quoted above. — [Certification news release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/california-secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-certifies-measures-november-3-2026-general-election-ballot)
- Page: “Keeps the current top income-tax rates after their scheduled 2031 expiration.” YES: “Existing higher rates on roughly the top 2% of earners become permanent.” NO: “Those higher rates expire in 2031; no one’s rate changes today.”

### Inferences
- Permanence versus 2031 expiration, and “no one’s rate changes today” on a no vote: ACCURATE. A yes vote also does not change rates today; it stops the 2031 expiration.
- “Roughly the top 2% of earners”: MOSTLY ACCURATE. The Analyst says “the top 2 percent of California taxpayers,” not “roughly” and not “earners.” The page omits the 89/11 school allocation, the ban on administrative costs, and the inflation-adjusted income thresholds.
- Short title “Permanent high-income tax rates” is not the official title, which leads with school and health-care funding. As a description of the tax mechanics it is fair.

### Gaps
- The VIG does not define the top 2 percent by a dollar cutoff in the same sentence as “top 2 percent.” The dollar thresholds and the “top 2 percent” sentence are both official, but they are not explicitly cross-walked.

## Proposition 4 — public campaign financing

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. A yes vote lets state and local governments create voluntary public-financing programs under spending and eligibility limits. The page’s no-vote exception says “charter cities” only. The official summary says charter cities and charter counties.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “REPEALS PROHIBITION AGAINST PUBLIC FUNDING OF ELECTION CAMPAIGNS. LEGISLATIVE STATUTE.” Attorney General: “Current law prohibits state and local governments from using public funds for election campaigns except in charter cities and counties.” “This measure repeals that prohibition and allows state and local governments to establish public campaign financing programs for candidates who agree to specified spending limits and meet eligibility criteria.” Also prohibits using funds “earmarked for education, transportation, or public safety,” and prohibits using public funds “for legal defense costs, fines, or repayment of personal loans to campaigns.” — [Prop 4 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “State and local governments could create public campaign financing programs for public office candidates, subject to limits on which public funds could be used, how the public funds could be used, and who could receive them.” Official no: “State and most local governments would remain unable to create public campaign finance programs for public office candidates.” — [Prop 4](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/4/)
- Page YES: “State and local governments may create programs under spending and eligibility rules.” Page NO: “The existing ban remains, except for charter cities with existing authority.”

### Inferences
- YES: ACCURATE as a compression of spending limits and eligibility criteria. It does not mention the earmark ban or the ban on legal-defense, fine, and personal-loan uses.
- NO: MOSTLY ACCURATE. The omitted condition is charter counties. The official text is “except in charter cities and counties,” and the official no box says “most local governments,” not charter cities alone.
- The measure allows governments to create programs. It does not itself appropriate campaign funds or require any government to create a program. The page’s “may” matches that.

### Gaps
- None on the charter-city-and-county exception.

## Proposition 5 — recall replacement process

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE on removing the same-ballot replacement vote, and under-inclusive on who is covered. The official title says “statewide officers,” and the page repeats that. The text amends Article II, section 15, and the Legislative Analyst applies the new vacancy rules to legislative seats as well as statewide offices. “Succession” is the Governor rule, and only in part.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “CHANGES RECALL ELECTION PROCESS FOR STATEWIDE OFFICERS. LEGISLATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT.” Attorney General: “Under current law, voters decide at the same election whether (1) to recall a state officer, and (2) who will replace that officer if recalled.” Under the measure: “if the Governor is recalled, the Lieutenant Governor becomes Governor for the remainder of the term or until a successor is elected”; “for all other recalled officers, the office will remain vacant until filled by a subsequent special election or appointment as specified in existing law”; “a recalled officer may be a candidate in a special election to fill the office.” — [Prop 5 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “Recall elections would no longer include a question about who should fill the vacancy. Instead, vacancies typically would be filled through special elections or appointments, depending on the office and timing.” Official no: “Voters would continue to be asked who should fill the vacancy as part of recall elections.” — [Prop 5](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/)
- Legislative Analyst: “Proposition 5 removes the second question—who should replace a recalled official—from a recall election.” “If a state official is recalled, Proposition 5 makes the office of the recalled official vacant. Except for the office of the Governor, Proposition 5 uses the existing process for filling vacancies.” “In the case of an office in the state Senate or Assembly, this means that a replacement typically would be elected through a separate special election. The Governor would choose who would fill a vacancy in any other office (other than office of the Governor).” If the Governor is recalled before the close of the nomination period for the next statewide election during the first two years of the term, “the Lieutenant Governor serves as Governor until voters elect a new Governor at a future statewide election.” If recalled later, “the Lieutenant Governor serves as Governor for the rest of the term.” — [Prop 5 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/5/analysis.htm)
- Text: SCA 1 amends Section 15 of Article II. New subdivision (c) deletes the plurality-successor rule and states that if the recall succeeds, “the office shall be vacant. The vacancy shall be filled in accordance with this Constitution and statute, provided that the officer who was the subject of the recall election shall not be appointed to fill the vacancy in that office.” New Article V, section 10(b): if the Governor is removed by recall, “the Lieutenant Governor shall become Governor for the remainder of the unexpired term,” but if removal is “before the close of the nomination period for the next statewide election during the first two years of the Governor’s term, a special election shall be called” and consolidated with the statewide primary and, if needed, the general election. — [Prop 5 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop5-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Page: “Removes the same-day replacement vote from recalls of statewide officers.” YES: “A successful recall creates a vacancy filled by succession, appointment, or a later special election.” NO: “Voters keep choosing removal and a replacement on the same ballot.”

### Inferences
- Removing the same-day replacement choice, and the no-vote description: ACCURATE relative to the official yes/no boxes.
- Limiting the one-liner to “statewide officers”: MOSTLY ACCURATE and under-inclusive. It matches the official title, but the amended section is Article II, section 15, and the Analyst expressly includes state Senate and Assembly vacancies (special election) and gubernatorial appointment for other non-Governor state offices.
- YES “succession, appointment, or a later special election”: MOSTLY ACCURATE. Succession to the Lieutenant Governor is the Governor rule, not a general succession rule for every statewide officer. Early in a gubernatorial term, the Lieutenant Governor serves until a later special election rather than for the whole remainder with no election. The recalled officer cannot be appointed to the vacancy; the Attorney General summary says that officer may still be a candidate in a special election. The page does not state that limit.

### Gaps
- The VIG title says “statewide officers” while the Analyst and section 15 text are written in terms of state officers, including legislators. This note does not resolve that labeling tension beyond quoting both. No separate official definition of “statewide” versus “state” officer was found inside the proposition text.

## Proposition 37 — middle-income homebuyer loans

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. CalHFA, up to $25 billion in borrower-repaid revenue bonds, fixed-rate loans, up to 17 percent of price, and a secondary mortgage are all in official sources. The page omits who and which homes qualify.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “CREATES LOAN PROGRAM FOR MIDDLE-INCOME BUYERS OF QUALIFIED NEW HOMES. INITIATIVE STATUTE.” Attorney General: “Authorizes up to $25 billion in bonds to offer eligible buyers fixed-rate mortgages for up to 17% of the purchase price of a ‘qualified new home’ (new construction or first sale of converted nonresidential property, priced below about $1 million–$1.5 million, depending on county, adjusted annually).” “Borrowers must be California residents for one year, occupy the home, earn less than twice the area’s median income, and pay at least 3% down.” “Requires that bonds be repaid by homeowners’ mortgage payments, not the State.” — [Prop 37 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “The state would create a new homebuying assistance program. The state could sell up to $25 billion in revenue bonds to fund the program. Bonds would be paid back by homeowners’ payments on their home loans.” Official no: “The state would not be required to create a new homebuying assistance program.” — [Prop 37](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/)
- Legislative Analyst: “Proposition 37 allows CalHFA to sell up to $25 billion in revenue bonds.” Each loan “would cover up to 17 percent of the home price.” Homebuyers “would need to provide a down payment of at least 3 percent.” “No direct state or local costs.” — [Prop 37 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/37/analysis.htm)
- Text: the California Housing Finance Agency administers the program. “Each middle-class home ownership loan secured by a secondary mortgage shall be subordinate to the primary mortgage.” The loan “shall finance no more than 17 percent of the purchase price.” Bonds are revenue bonds. The principal amount “shall not exceed twenty-five billion dollars ($25,000,000,000).” The word “fixed” appears in the Attorney General summary; the operative loan section reviewed sets interest rates by agency rule rather than using the words “fixed rate” in that subsection. — [Prop 37 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop37-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Page: “Authorizes up to $25B in borrower-repaid revenue bonds for second mortgages on qualifying new homes.” YES: “CalHFA may offer fixed-rate second mortgages up to 17% of a qualifying new home’s price.” NO: “The program is not created.”

### Inferences
- $25 billion, revenue bonds, borrower repayment, CalHFA, 17 percent, qualifying new homes, and “second mortgage” as a secondary mortgage subordinate to the primary mortgage: ACCURATE.
- “Fixed-rate”: ACCURATE to the Attorney General title and summary, which uses those words. The operative subsection that was read authorizes the agency to set interest rates; it does not separately quote a fixed-rate mandate in that subsection.
- Overall: MOSTLY ACCURATE because the page omits material conditions: one-year residency, occupancy, income under 200 percent of area median, at least 3 percent down, and county price caps of about $1 million to $1.5 million. The no box’s “not required to create” is slightly softer than the page’s “is not created,” but a no vote means the program is not established. That difference is not material.

### Gaps
- Whether every agency-set rate must be fixed for the life of the loan is stated in the Attorney General summary and not re-quoted from the rate-setting subsection.

## Proposition 38 — immunology research bond

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. The $8.4 billion general-obligation authority and the yes/no borrow choice match. The page’s “split” omits that the split is equal, that half the research money goes to three named diseases, and that a 20 percent in-California price discount applies.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “AUTHORIZES BONDS FOR IMMUNOLOGY MEDICAL RESEARCH. INITIATIVE STATUTE.” Attorney General: “Authorizes $8.4 billion in state general obligation bonds for immunology and immunotherapy research … with half dedicated to cancer, heart disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.” “Allocates funds equally between (1) a single University of California-affiliated nonprofit medical research institute, and (2) research grants for public or nonprofit universities and institutions.” “Requires funding recipients to sell technology and drugs derived from research in California for 20% below national average price.” — [Prop 38 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “The state could borrow $8.4 billion to support medical research in immunology and immunotherapy conducted by California research institutes and universities.” Official no: “The state could not borrow $8.4 billion to support medical research in immunology and immunotherapy conducted by California research institutes and universities.” — [Prop 38](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/38/)
- Page: “Borrow $8.4B for immune-system research, split between one institute and a grant program.” YES: “California may issue $8.4B in bonds for immunology and immunotherapy research.” NO: “The state may not issue these bonds.”

### Inferences
- Amount, subject matter, two-way split, and yes/no borrow choice: ACCURATE.
- One-liner: MOSTLY ACCURATE. Omitted conditions that are in the official summary: equal allocation; the institute must be one UC-affiliated nonprofit chosen under the measure; half of research money for cancer, heart disease, and Alzheimer’s; 20 percent below the national average price in California. The official yes box also limits the research to California institutes and universities. The page’s “may issue” matches “could borrow.”

### Gaps
- None on the $8.4 billion authorization.

## Proposition 39 — voter identification

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE, and the mail-ballot rule is underspecified. In-person voting requires government identification. Mail voting requires last-four digits that match the identification number the voter designated at registration; a mismatch invalidates the ballot. The page does not say that. It also omits the annual citizenship-verification report. The word “primarily” is not in the official no box.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “PROHIBITS CITIZENS FROM VOTING UNLESS THEY PRESENT GOVERNMENT-ISSUED IDENTIFICATION. INITIATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT.” VIG Attorney General summary: “Prohibits voting by mail unless voter designates a government-issued identification number for their voter registration.” “Invalidates mail ballots if envelope does not include last four digits of a government-issued identification number matching the number designated in the voter’s registration (e.g., if voter designates Social Security number for registration, but provides driver’s license number on envelope, their ballot would not count).” “Prohibits voting in person without presenting government-issued identification.” “Requires elections officials to annually report percentage of each county’s voters whose citizenship status they have verified.” — [Prop 39 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “Voters would be required to present additional identifying information to elections officials each time they vote.” Official no: “A voter’s identity would continue to be established with the voter’s signature.” — [Prop 39](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/)
- The June 25, 2026 certification summary was broader and different in detail: present government identification at the polls “or the last four digits of a government-issued identification number when voting by mail”; “the State provide voter identification cards on request”; and an annual citizenship-verification percentage report. The VIG summary quoted above does not mention state-issued voter identification cards and adds the designation-and-match rule and express invalidation. — [Certification news release](https://www.sos.ca.gov/administration/news-releases-and-advisories/2026-news-releases-and-advisories/california-secretary-state-shirley-n-weber-phd-certifies-measures-november-3-2026-general-election-ballot)
- Page: “Requires government ID in person and ID digits on mail-ballot envelopes.” YES: “Voters provide additional identifying information every time they vote.” NO: “Identity continues to be established primarily through the voter’s signature.”

### Inferences
- YES, compared with the official vote-means box: ACCURATE.

## Proposition 40 — one-time billionaire wealth tax

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. It is a one-time tax of up to 5 percent, aimed at covered wealth over $1 billion, with 90 percent for health care. The legal base is 5 percent of net worth, not only the dollars above $1 billion, with a rate phase-down between $1 billion and $1.1 billion. Real property and some retirement assets are excluded. The tax is keyed to California residency on January 1, 2026. The page’s yes line drops “up to,” which the official vote-means box also drops.

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “IMPOSES ONE-TIME TAX ON CERTAIN TAXPAYERS. INITIATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT AND STATUTE.” VIG Attorney General summary: “Imposes one-time tax of up to 5% on certain taxpayers with covered assets valued over $1 billion; covered assets include businesses, securities, art, collectibles, and intellectual property, but exclude real property and some pensions and retirement accounts.” “Allocates 90% of these tax revenues for health care, 10% for food assistance or education-related programs; prohibits using revenues to replace existing funding for these purposes.” “Exempts such tax revenues from constitutional requirements for school funding, budget reserves, and state spending limit.” — [Prop 40 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “The state would collect a one-time tax from billionaires equal to 5 percent of their wealth.” Official no: “The state would not collect a one-time tax from billionaires equal to 5 percent of their wealth.” — [Prop 40](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/)
- Legislative Analyst: “billionaires who were residents of California on January 1, 2026 would have to pay a one-time state tax equal to 5 percent of their net worth. The tax would be due in 2027.” Taxpayers may spread payment over five years “but would have to pay more to do so.” “Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts generally would be excluded.” “Ninety percent of the money would have to be spent on health care services for the public. The rest would have to be spent on education, food assistance, and administration of the wealth tax.” Spending-limit and school-funding rules “would not apply to this money.” — [Prop 40 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm)
- Statute, section 50301: an excise tax “for tax year 2026” on “applicable individuals with net worth of one billion dollars ($1,000,000,000) or more, and on applicable trusts.” “The tax imposed is 5 percent of the net worth of such individual or trust.” If an individual other than a trust has net worth under “one billion one hundred million dollars ($1,100,000,000),” the tax “shall be reduced by 0.1 percentage point, but not below zero, for each two million dollars ($2,000,000)” below $1.1 billion. That phase-down reaches a zero rate at $1.0 billion (50 steps of $2 million, times 0.1 percentage point, equals 5 percentage points). Optional deferral: five installments with “an annual nondeductible deferral charge of 7.5 percent of the remaining unpaid balance.” Chapter title: “IMPOSITION OF TAX ON PERSONAL WEALTH IN EXCESS OF $1 BILLION IN NET WORTH.” — [Prop 40 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Constitutional spending-limit exclusion in the same text: Reserve Fund revenue “shall not be considered” proceeds of taxes under Section 8 or 8.5 of Article XVI or under Article XIII B, and “‘Appropriations subject to limitation’ of each entity of government and the state under Article XIII B shall not include appropriations of revenue from the Reserve Fund.”
- Page one-liner: “Taxes covered wealth above $1B at up to 5%, mainly for health care.” YES: “California collects a one-time 5% wealth tax from covered billionaires and trusts.” NO: “The one-time wealth tax is not collected.”

### Inferences
- “Up to 5%,” “above $1 billion,” “covered” assets, and “mainly for health care” (90 percent): MOSTLY ACCURATE. They track the Attorney General summary. The risk of misreading is that “wealth above $1B” can sound like a tax only on the slice over $1 billion. The statute taxes 5 percent of net worth (subject to the phase-down and exclusions), so a person at $1.1 billion is taxed at 5 percent of net worth, not 5 percent of $100 million.
- YES “a one-time 5% wealth tax”: MOSTLY ACCURATE. It matches the official vote-means sentence, which also says “equal to 5 percent,” and it correctly adds trusts, which section 50301 taxes. It drops the summary’s “up to,” the $1.0-to-$1.1 billion phase-down, the real-property and retirement exclusions, the January 1, 2026 residency date, and the five-year deferral with a 7.5 percent charge.
- NO: ACCURATE. If Prop 40 is not approved, the tax is not imposed. Interaction with Props 41 and 42 is a separate question below; a yes on 40 alone does not guarantee collection.
- “Mainly for health care” does not state the non-supplantation rule or the exemption from school-funding, reserve, and spending-limit rules. Those are in the official summary and matter for Prop 41.

### Gaps
- The valuation date as distinct from the January 1, 2026 residency date in the Analyst’s sentence was not separately quoted from the statute. The residency finding is the Analyst’s.

## Proposition 42 — personal-property and retroactive taxes

### Takeaway
MOSTLY ACCURATE. The ban covers new state taxes on owning personal property, including financial assets, and limits new retroactive taxes, with emergency exceptions. The page’s date “enacted after Jan. 1, 2026” is narrower than the official summary’s “enacted or take effect on or after January 1, 2026.” “Enacted” is not “qualified.”

### Cited Findings
- Official title: “PROHIBITS NEW STATE PERSONAL PROPERTY TAXES AND CERTAIN RETROACTIVE STATE TAXES. INITIATIVE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT.” Attorney General: “Prohibits new state taxes on personal property, including business interests, intellectual property, and financial assets (including assets in retirement and investment accounts).” “Prohibits new state taxes that apply retroactively based on the taxpayer’s conduct, activities, or a status that occurred before the new tax’s effective date.” “Applies to new taxes that are enacted or take effect on or after January 1, 2026.” “States that other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes.” — [Prop 42 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm)
- Official yes: “The state could not establish new taxes on the ownership of financial assets or other personal property.” Official no: “The state would continue to have the option to establish new taxes on the ownership of financial assets or other personal property.” The vote-means box does not mention retroactive taxes. The title and summary do. — [Prop 42](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/)
- Legislative Analyst: “new taxes on the ownership of financial assets or other personal property would not be allowed.” “Proposition 42 limits the situations that a ballot measure or the Legislature can raise any tax retroactively.” — [Prop 42 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/analysis.htm)

Real-property taxation under other constitutional provisions is not altered. Taxes “in effect, imposed, and first collected on or before December 31, 2025” are not altered. Retroactivity ban: no such new law may impose a tax “that operates retroactively to result in tax liability based on conduct, activities, or a status that occurred or was present prior to the effective date,” including “a tax that is imposed on a taxpayer based upon their residency status on a date prior to the effective date of the tax.” Exception: a statute passed by the Legislature on or after January 1, 2026 may reach back no more than 365 days if the Governor declares a listed emergency or a fiscal emergency and the revenue is limited to that response. ### Inferences
- “Most retroactive taxes”: ACCURATE as a hedge. The statute has a 365-day legislative exception for declared emergencies and fiscal emergencies, and it preserves taxes already collected on or before December 31, 2025. It is not a ban on every retroactive tax.
- Date: MOSTLY ACCURATE, and the page is short of the official formulation. The summary says “enacted or take effect on or after January 1, 2026,” not “enacted after.” The statute’s prohibition uses “enacted on or after January 1, 2026.” The application section also covers initiatives “submitted to the electors on or after January 1, 2026,” including measures on the same ballot. “After” drops January 1 itself. “Enacted” in these texts is not defined as the date a petition qualified. An initiative qualified before January 1, 2026 is still covered if it is submitted to the electors on or after that date or is enacted or takes effect on or after that date. Prop 40 was part of the June 25, 2026 certification, so it was not qualified before January 1, 2026.
- “Conflicting taxes … are voided” in the yes sentence, read alone: MOSTLY ACCURATE. The page’s separate conflict rule states the vote comparison. The statute voids “all the provisions of the other measure,” not merely the conflicting sentence.
- NO: MOSTLY ACCURATE. The official no box mentions only personal-property taxes. The title and summary also cover retroactive taxes, so “those future tax options” is a fair reading of a no vote on the whole measure.

### Gaps
- No official definition equating “enacted” with “qualified” or with “submitted to the electors” was found beyond the sections quoted. The application section is the clearest text on initiatives that were in process before the election.

## Do Props 41 and 42 block Prop 40 if they get more yes votes?

### Takeaway
The page’s rule is mostly right and incomplete. California Constitution article II, section 10(b) gives conflicting provisions of the approved measure with the highest number of affirmative votes priority. It is not “later enacted,” not the higher proposition number, and not the higher yes percentage. Props 41 and 42 go further: if they pass and receive more affirmative votes than a conflicting initiative, they void that entire other measure. Prop 40 is written to fall under both of those clauses. Prop 40’s own conflict clause is narrower and does not clearly name Props 41 and 42. A measure that is not approved does not block anything, even if it received more yes votes.

### Cited Findings
- California Constitution, article II, section 10(b): “If provisions of two or more measures approved at the same election conflict, the provisions of the measure receiving the highest number of affirmative votes shall prevail.” Section 10(a): an approved initiative statute or referendum “takes effect on the fifth day after the Secretary of State files the statement of the vote,” unless the measure provides a later operative date. — [leginfo, article II, section 10](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CONS&sectionNum=SEC.%2010.&article=II)
- Prop 40 conflict clause, section 8 of the text: “This act provides for a tax on net worth, not income, of billionaires. In the event that this measure and another measure that levies a tax or affects the tax rates on net worth, not income, of billionaires, or that provides for use of funds from a tax on billionaire net worth, shall appear on the same statewide ballot, the provisions of the other measure or measures shall be deemed to be in conflict with this measure. In the event that this measure receives a greater number of affirmative votes than a measure deemed to be in conflict with it, the provisions of this measure shall prevail in their entirety, and any conflicting provisions of the other measure or measures shall be null and void.” That clause deems a conflict only for measures that tax or change rates on billionaire net worth, or that direct the use of billionaire-net-worth tax funds. It then voids only “conflicting provisions” of that other measure. — [Prop 40 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-text-proposed-laws.pdf)
- Prop 40 is the kind of tax Prop 41 describes. The Attorney General summary says Prop 40 “Exempts such tax revenues from constitutional requirements for school funding, budget reserves, and state spending limit.” The text says Reserve Fund appropriations are not “appropriations subject to limitation” under Article XIII B. Prop 41, section 3, says the state “shall not impose, collect, or enforce” a state tax enacted or taking effect on or after January 1, 2026 if it is excluded from the Government Spending Limitation. Prop 41, conflicting-measures section: if another initiative on the same ballot “(B) contains a state tax that is exempted or excluded from the Government Spending Limitation,” it “shall be deemed to be in conflict with this measure.” If Prop 41 “receives a greater number of affirmative votes, the provisions of this measure shall prevail in their entirety, and all the provisions of the other measure shall be null and void.” Prop 41 also says it “applies to all statewide initiative measures submitted to the electors on or after January 1, 2026, including measures that appear on the ballot at the same election.” — [Prop 41 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop41-text-proposed-laws.pdf); [Prop 40 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm)
- Prop 40 is also the kind of tax Prop 42 describes. Section 50301 taxes net worth, and the constitutional text “provides for taxation on all forms of personal property and wealth.” Prop 42’s definition of protected property includes financial assets, business interests, and intellectual property. Separately, the Legislative Analyst says the tax applies to “billionaires who were residents of California on January 1, 2026,” a residency status date before the election and before the article II, section 10 effective date. Prop 42, section 2(a)(2), covers “a tax that is imposed on a taxpayer based upon their residency status on a date prior to the effective date of the tax.” Prop 42’s conflict clause: if another initiative on the same ballot would impose a tax on ownership or control of the defined personal savings or property, or would enact a tax based on conduct, activities, or status before its effective date, that initiative is deemed in conflict. If Prop 42 receives “a greater number of affirmative votes,” its provisions “prevail in their entirety, and all the provisions of the other measure shall be null and void.” — [Prop 42 text](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf); [Prop 40 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm)
- Attorney General summaries for both 41 and 42: “other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes.” — [Prop 41 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/title-summary.htm); [Prop 42 title and summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm)
- Page claims checked: “If conflicting measures pass, the one with more Yes votes controls.” Prop 41 fail-state: “Prop 41 cannot block Prop 40; Prop 42 can still do so if it passes with more Yes votes.” Prop 42 fail-state is the inverse. Prop 40 pass-flow: revenue is $0 if 41 or 42 has more yes votes.

### Inferences
- “More Yes votes” as the conflict rule: MOSTLY ACCURATE. The constitution says the highest number of affirmative votes, and only among measures that are approved. It does not say the highest yes percentage. It does not say the later-enacted or higher-numbered measure wins. Section 10(b) prefers conflicting provisions, not automatically the entire losing measure.
- Prop 41 can void Prop 40: ACCURATE if both are approved and Prop 41 receives more affirmative votes than Prop 40. The mechanism is Prop 41’s own clause, which nullifies all provisions of an initiative that contains a state tax excluded from the spending limit. Prop 40’s spending-limit exemption is explicit. It is not only a partial severance of the exemption. The Analyst’s “might not” on the spending limit is a hedge in the analysis; the conflict clause itself says the other measure is null and void.
- Prop 42 can void Prop 40: ACCURATE on the same both-approved, more-affirmative-votes condition. Two independent hooks are in the official record: the tax is on personal property and net worth, and the Analyst ties liability to residency on January 1, 2026, before the effective date. Either hook is what Prop 42’s conflict clause deems a conflict. The result on the face of Prop 42 is that all of Prop 40 is null and void, so that tax raises no revenue.
- Fail-state symmetry: ACCURATE. If Prop 41 is not approved, it has no legal effect and cannot block Prop 40. Prop 42 can still do so if Prop 42 is approved and has more affirmative votes. The inverse is also true. Each blocker is independent.
- “Revenue is $0 if 41 or 42 has more Yes votes”: MOSTLY ACCURATE. It is right when that measure is also approved (the page’s general rule says “if conflicting measures pass”) and the vote comparison is affirmative votes, not percentages. It is not right if 41 or 42 received more yes votes but failed. It is also not a rule that any extra yes vote on an unrelated provision reduces revenue; the texts void the whole of Prop 40.
- If Prop 40 is approved and receives more affirmative votes than 41 and than 42, section 10(b) says Prop 40’s conflicting provisions prevail. Prop 40’s own clause would not clearly deem 41 or 42 “in conflict,” because those measures do not levy a billionaire net-worth tax or earmark its proceeds. The constitutional provision, not Prop 40’s deeming clause, is what still lets the higher affirmative vote on Prop 40 prevail over conflicting provisions of 41 or 42. The page’s “the one with more Yes votes controls” covers that result. It does not explain this asymmetry.
- Prop 42 does not void taxes merely because they were qualified before January 1, 2026. See the Proposition 42 section. Same-ballot application is express.

### Gaps
- Article II, section 10(b) does not state what happens if two approved conflicting measures receive the same number of affirmative votes. No tie-breaker was found in that section.
- This note does not rely on case law as a primary source. Secondary context only: the California Supreme Court has read section 10(b), for competing comprehensive schemes and absent a contrary intent in the measures, to enforce only the measure with the highest affirmative vote rather than to splice the measures together. Props 41 and 42 state their own intent: the other measure is null and void in full. That case was not re-read for this note and is not needed for the statutory result above.
- Whether a court would accept every theory on which Prop 42 calls Prop 40 retroactive, beyond the residency sentence in the Legislative Analyst’s analysis, is not decided by these sources. The personal-property conflict does not depend on that theory.
