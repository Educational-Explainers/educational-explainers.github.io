# Fiscal and mechanical fact check: California November 3, 2026 Propositions 39–45

Scope is the claims in `ca-2026-ballot/index.html` (snapshot dated October 5, 2026) against the official voter-guide title and summary, the Legislative Analyst’s Office (LAO) ballot analyses, and, where the summaries conflict or omit a mechanism, the proposed-law descriptions those official pages point to. Campaign arguments are separated from LAO findings. Verdicts: ACCURATE, MOSTLY ACCURATE, INACCURATE, MISLEADING, or UNVERIFIED.

The page states: “Vote effects and fiscal estimates come from California's official Voter Information Guide and the nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office.” Several verbal ranges do. The numeric bar endpoints $20 million, $300 million, $80 million, and $110 million, and the derived “0.01–0.12% of the General Fund,” do not appear in either source.

## Does the sourcing claim hold, and does Prop 40’s “official fiscal analysis” link contain the LAO analysis?

### Takeaway
The verbal LAO ranges for these seven measures are real and are quoted fairly in the cards’ cost lines, with two important exceptions: Prop 39’s General Fund percentage and Prop 45’s bar convert unbounded LAO language into precise dollar endpoints that the LAO never published. Prop 40’s “Official fiscal analysis” link opens the Secretary of State quick guide, which reprints the ballot-label fiscal blurb and the yes/no statements but not the LAO narrative.

### Cited Findings
- Prop 40’s card sets `lao` to `https://quickguidetoprops.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2026-11-03/40`, and the UI labels that URL “Official fiscal analysis.” The same URL is used as the Secretary of State link. Source: `ca-2026-ballot/index.html` (proposition data, n:40).
- That quick-guide page contains the official summary line and this fiscal blurb: “Temporary revenue increase of tens of billions of dollars spread over several years from wealth tax on billionaires. Possible ongoing decrease of less than $1 billion per year in income tax revenue from billionaires.” It also prints the yes/no statements. It does not print the LAO background, the 90 percent allocation narrative, the administration-cost paragraph, or the conflict paragraph. It tells readers to “Read the California Voter Information Guide” at `http://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/` rather than linking the LAO analysis. — [SOS quick guide, Prop 40](https://quickguidetoprops.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2026-11-03/40)
- The full LAO analysis is at [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026), PDF [prop40-110326.pdf](https://lao.ca.gov/ballot/2026/prop40-110326.pdf), and in the voter guide at [Voter guide Prop 40 analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm) and [title and summary PDF](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-title-summ-analysis.pdf).
- Props 41–45 on the page do link to the LAO HTML analyses. Prop 39 links to the LAO PDF `https://lao.ca.gov/ballot/2026/prop39-110326.pdf`, which matches the HTML analysis. — `index.html`; [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026)
- The LAO fiscal summaries use “tens of billions,” “less than $1 billion per year,” “tens of millions … to low hundreds of millions,” “low millions,” “low tens of millions,” and “high tens of millions … potentially exceeding $100 million.” They do not publish $20 million, $300 million, $80 million, $110 million, 0.01 percent, or 0.12 percent. — LAO analyses linked above and voter-guide title pages cited under each proposition.

### Inferences
- A reader who clicks “Official fiscal analysis” on Prop 40 never reaches the LAO write-up unless they navigate onward. The two headline fiscal sentences are nevertheless on that page, because they are the ballot label.
- Any figure on the page that is only a translation of LAO words into a bar endpoint is unsourced even when the words themselves are sourced.

### Gaps
- The full proposed-law PDFs were not line-read for every operative section (notably the Prop 40 rate formula and any Prop 40 findings about federal cuts). Where the Attorney General summary and the LAO analysis already conflict, that conflict is reported rather than resolved by a secondary paraphrase.

## Proposition 39 — voter identification

### Takeaway
The identification rules and the verbal cost range match the LAO and the Attorney General summary. The “0.01–0.12% of the General Fund” figure and the bar endpoints of $20 million and $300 million are the page’s own arithmetic, and labeling combined state-and-local costs as a General Fund share is misleading. The fraud sentence is an uncited argument, not an LAO finding.

### Cited Findings
- One-line claim: “Requires government ID in person and ID digits on mail-ballot envelopes.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “Prohibits voting in person without presenting government-issued identification.” “Invalidates mail ballots if envelope does not include last four digits of a government-issued identification number matching the number designated in the voter’s registration (e.g., if voter designates Social Security number for registration, but provides driver’s license number on envelope, their ballot would not count).” LAO: in person, “present government-issued identification”; by mail, “the last four digits of their selected government-issued identification number on the ballot envelope.” — [Voter guide Prop 39 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026)
- Yes-vote claim: “Voters provide additional identifying information every time they vote.” **ACCURATE.** Official yes statement: “Voters would be required to present additional identifying information to elections officials each time they vote.” LAO: “each time they cast a ballot in any election in California.” — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026); [quick reference](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/quick-reference-guide/39.htm)
- No-vote claim: “Identity continues to be established primarily through the voter’s signature.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Official no statement, word for word: “A voter’s identity would continue to be established with the voter’s signature.” The page adds “primarily,” which is not in the official sentence. LAO background does say in-person voters “typically identify themselves by signing” a roster and mail voters by signing the envelope. — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026)
- Cost claim: “Tens to low hundreds of millions each year; roughly 0.01–0.12% of the General Fund.” Split verdict. The words **ACCURATE.** The percentage **MISLEADING.** LAO fiscal effects: “Proposition 39 would increase state and local government costs each year by tens of millions of dollars to low hundreds of millions of dollars. These costs represent less than one-quarter of 1 percent (0.25 percent) of the state’s total General Fund budget.” Fiscal summary: “State and local government costs of between tens of millions of dollars and low hundreds of millions of dollars each year.” The LAO never says 0.01 percent or 0.12 percent. — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026)
- Bar data: `{prop:39, low:.02, high:.3, label:'Tens to low hundreds of millions / year'}`. The percent function divides by the sum of the page’s General Fund spending categories, which equals $251.477214 billion, i.e. the page’s “$251.477B” General Fund. — `ca-2026-ballot/index.html` lines defining `annualImpacts`, `budget2026.spending`, `budget2026.allFunds`, and `pct`.
- “Adds an objective identity check and requires a free state voter-ID option.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE** as a pro-box argument. “Objective” is not official language. The free card is official. LAO: “Proposition 39 requires the state to provide a voter identification card at no cost to voters who request one.” The official pro argument says the measure “guarantees every voter a FREE Voter ID if they want one.” — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026); [arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/arguments-rebuttals.htm)
- “Mandates regular citizenship-verification reporting and audits.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** LAO: elections officials must “report every year the percentage of registered voters in each county whose citizenship status has been verified,” using “best efforts” and government data, not a guarantee that every registration is verified. Separately, “In every odd-numbered year,” the State Auditor reports “how well the state and each county comply with the measure.” The audit is of compliance with the whole measure, not a citizenship-only audit. The Attorney General bullet states the annual citizenship report and does not mention the auditor. — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026); [title summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/title-summary.htm)
- “Adds significant recurring cost without evidence of widespread voter fraud.” **Uncited argument, not a sourced finding.** It sits in “Best case against.” The method note says pros and cons are “the best good-faith cases made by each side.” The LAO analysis and the Attorney General summary do not discuss fraud incidence at all. The official “Argument Against” likewise does not say there is no evidence of widespread fraud; it calls the measure “costly, unnecessary barriers” and says it would “waste hundreds of millions in taxpayer dollars.” — [LAO Prop 39](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=39&year=2026); [arguments](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/39/arguments-rebuttals.htm); [quick reference con](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/quick-reference-guide/39.htm)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic the page implies but does not print: low 0.02 means $20 million; high 0.3 means $300 million. “Tens of millions” can include $20 million. “Low hundreds of millions” can be read as topping out around $300 million, but that ceiling is the page’s, not the LAO’s. The only official scalar is “less than … 0.25 percent” of the General Fund. Using the page’s $251.477 billion General Fund, 0.25 percent is about $629 million. The bar’s $300 million high is inside that LAO ceiling and narrower than it.
- Displayed percentages: `pct(0.02) = 0.02 / 251.477214 × 100 = 0.00795 percent`, which `toFixed(2)` renders as **0.01%**. `pct(0.3) = 0.1193 percent`, rendered as **0.12%**. The user’s check is right: $20 million / $251,477 million is about 0.008 percent, not 0.01 percent, and $300 million / $251,477 million is about 0.12 percent. The card’s “roughly 0.01–0.12%” matches the rounded display, not the unrounded low end. “Roughly” covers a 0.002-point rounding gap; it does not make 0.01–0.12 an LAO figure.
- Who pays: the LAO says “state and local government costs” and then compares that combined amount with the state General Fund. The page’s fiscal-overview header is “Largest yearly General Fund effects,” and the bar prints the result as “% GF.” That treats a state-plus-county cost as if it were a General Fund outlay on the same axis as General Fund debt service (Props 1 and 38). The LAO does not say what share counties would pay, so the General Fund percentage is not a measured GF appropriation. It is misleading as a GF-cost claim. It is not inaccurate as a restatement of the LAO’s own “less than 0.25 percent of the General Fund” scale comparison — except that the page substitutes an unsourced 0.01–0.12 band for the LAO’s “less than 0.25 percent.”
- Omitted mechanics that change how the rule works, not the dollar range: a mismatched ID type voids the mail ballot (Social Security digits versus driver-license digits is the Attorney General’s example); only the last four digits count; voters must designate an ID number in order to vote by mail; the Legislature must still define which IDs qualify and what “best efforts” at citizenship verification means; some savings from a smaller voter roll are possible and “likely would not exceed the costs.”

### Gaps
- No official state-versus-county split of the cost was found, so “much of the cost is county-funded” cannot be confirmed or denied. The misleading part is presenting an unknown mix as a General Fund percentage with two-decimal precision.

## Proposition 40 — one-time wealth tax

### Takeaway
“Tens of billions” over several years and a possible ongoing income-tax loss under $1 billion a year are the LAO’s own words; the LAO published no narrower range. The rate is not a simple 5 percent tax on the slice of wealth above $1 billion. The 90/10 health split and the ban on replacing existing funding are in the Attorney General summary. “After federal cuts” is campaign rationale, not an LAO or title-and-summary finding. Voiding the tax if Prop 41 or 42 gets more yes votes is a real conflict rule, but the LAO says the courts “could” find a conflict, not that revenue is automatically $0.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Taxes covered wealth above $1B at up to 5%, mainly for health care.” **MISLEADING** on the base; the “up to 5%” and “mainly for health care” pieces track the Attorney General. Official title: “Imposes one-time tax of up to 5% on certain taxpayers with covered assets valued over $1 billion; covered assets include businesses, securities, art, collectibles, and intellectual property, but exclude real property and some pensions and retirement accounts.” “Allocates 90% of these tax revenues for health care, 10% for food assistance or education-related programs.” The tax is described as imposed on taxpayers whose covered assets exceed $1 billion, at a rate of up to 5 percent — not as a tax whose base is only the dollars above $1 billion. — [Voter guide Prop 40 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm); [title/analysis PDF, p. 54](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-title-summ-analysis.pdf)
- LAO, same voter guide: “billionaires who were residents of California on January 1, 2026 would have to pay a one-time state tax equal to 5 percent of their net worth.” “Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts generally would be excluded from the tax.” Official yes statement: “The state would collect a one-time tax from billionaires equal to 5 percent of their wealth.” — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026); [VIG analysis](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/analysis.htm)
- Yes-line: “California collects a one-time 5% wealth tax from covered billionaires and trusts.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE**, with two drifts. It matches the LAO yes statement’s “5 percent” and drops the Attorney General’s “up to.” It adds “trusts,” which the LAO yes/no statements and the LAO narrative do not mention; the Attorney General says “certain taxpayers,” not “trusts.” Secondary readings of the proposed law say applicable trusts are taxed, and at least one says a non-grantor applicable trust is taxed on its net worth without its own $1 billion threshold. Those trust details are **not** LAO findings. — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026); [Holland & Knight, Sept. 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/californias-proposed-billionaire-tax-what-you-need-to-know); [GrowSF annotated text, § 50308](https://growsf.org/voter-guide/san-francisco-voter-guide-november-2026-election/measures/prop-40/legal-text/)
- Phase-in, from secondary readings of the text, not from the LAO: full 5 percent at $1.1 billion and above; between $1 billion and $1.1 billion the rate falls by 0.1 percentage point for each $2 million below $1.1 billion, to zero at $1 billion. SPUR’s illustration: $1.08 billion pays 4 percent, $1.06 billion pays 3 percent, $1.04 billion pays 2 percent, $1.02 billion pays 1 percent. Hoover: “The rate applies to the whole fortune, not just the amount above the threshold.” The LAO analysis does not spell out this phase-in; the Attorney General’s “up to 5%” is the official trace of it. — [SPUR](https://www.spur.org/voter-guide/2026-11/ca-prop-40-billionaire-tax); [Hoover Proposition Lab](https://www.hoover.org/proposition-lab-proposition-40); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/09/californias-proposed-billionaire-tax-what-you-need-to-know)
- Cost line: “LAO: tens of billions over several years, with a possible ongoing income-tax loss under $1B a year.” **ACCURATE.** LAO: “The state probably would collect tens of billions of dollars from the wealth tax. This temporary increase in state tax collections would be spread across several years.” “These responses probably would reduce state revenues by less than $1 billion per year.” Fiscal summary uses the same two sentences. No point estimate and no range such as $20–40 billion appears in the LAO ballot analysis or the voter-guide fiscal summary. The page’s “tens of billions” is the official range, not a vaguer substitute for a tighter official number. — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- Annual-impact bar: `{prop:40, low:0, high:1, label:'Potential ongoing revenue loss under $1B / year', uncertain:true}`. **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** It charts the ongoing income-tax loss, not the one-time revenue. LAO’s phrase is “less than $1 billion per year,” so a bar whose high end is exactly $1 billion reaches a line the LAO says the loss is under. The label says “under.” The impact note says this risk is separate from direct annual costs. That matches the code: uncertain items are excluded from `maxDirectCost`. — `index.html`; [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- Big-number card: “Tens of billions · Potential one-time revenue. Collected over several years only if Prop 40 wins its conflict with Props 41 and 42.” The revenue words are **ACCURATE.** “Only if” is **stronger than the LAO.** LAO: “If Proposition 41 or 42 on this same ballot receive more ‘yes’ votes than Proposition 40, then Proposition 40 could be stopped from becoming law even if it gets yes votes from a majority of voters. This is because the courts could find that Proposition 41 or 42 conflict with Proposition 40.” — [VIG PDF p. 55](https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-title-summ-analysis.pdf)
- Flow: “90% health · 10% food/education.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “90% … for health care, 10% for food assistance or education-related programs.” LAO: “Ninety percent of the money would have to be spent on health care services for the public. The rest would have to be spent on education, food assistance, and administration of the wealth tax.” The page follows the Attorney General and omits the LAO’s inclusion of tax administration inside the non-health share. The Attorney General’s “or” does not require a fixed split between food and education. — [title summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- “Raises large one-time revenue mainly for health care after federal cuts.” Revenue and health-care use: **ACCURATE** as to the measure. “After federal cuts”: **not an LAO or title-and-summary fact.** Neither the Attorney General bullets nor the LAO analysis condition the tax, the 90 percent, or the revenue estimate on federal cuts. The quick-guide pro argument does: “Trump slashed healthcare funding to pay for billionaire tax breaks, eliminating coverage for over a million Californians…” The sentence is in the page’s “Best case for” box. It is a campaign rationale presented as an argument, not as an LAO finding. — [SOS quick guide pro argument](https://quickguidetoprops.sos.ca.gov/propositions/2026-11-03/40); [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- “Targets extreme wealth and bars replacement of existing program funding.” The non-supplant clause is **ACCURATE** and is official, not only a talking point. Attorney General: “prohibits using revenues to replace existing funding for these purposes.” “Extreme wealth” is characterization; the legal threshold in the title is covered assets valued over $1 billion. — [title summary](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm)
- “Revenue is uncertain and could encourage wealthy taxpayers to leave.” **ACCURATE** as a paraphrase of the LAO, and it is in the con box. LAO: “Exactly when and how much the state would collect is very hard to predict.” “Billionaires may respond to a new wealth tax in ways that reduce their income tax payments. For example, some billionaires may decide to leave California.” The LAO then bounds “these responses” at “less than $1 billion per year.” Departure is an example, not a forecast that taxpayers will leave. — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- “One-time money does not permanently fund ongoing health programs.” **Not LAO language.** It is a con-box inference. The LAO says the revenue increase is temporary and spread over several years, and that the state decides how and when to spend it within the required uses. The LAO does not say the money cannot support health programs for as long as it lasts, and it does not say the programs would be unfunded after the money is gone. — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026)
- Conflict and “$0 if Prop 41 or 42 passes with more Yes votes.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE, over-definite.** See the LAO “could” / “courts could find” quote above. Props 41 and 42 each also contain their own clause, in the Attorney General summary: “States that other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes.” The page’s flow says the tax “is void” and revenue is “$0.” That is what those measures say **if** a conflict exists and the other measure has more yes votes. The LAO does not treat the conflict as already adjudicated. — [LAO Prop 40](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=40&year=2026); [Prop 41 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/title-summary.htm); [Prop 42 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm)

### Inferences
- Scale of the “above $1B” ambiguity: LAO says 5 percent of net worth, not 5 percent of the excess over $1 billion. At a $2 billion net worth those readings differ by about half ($100 million versus $50 million). Near $1.1 billion they differ by an order of magnitude if the phase-in is ignored. Excluding real property and some retirement accounts, which both official summaries state and the page’s one-liner does not, also changes the base.
- Omitted LAO cost that is real but not a General Fund hit: “These costs could be tens of millions of dollars per year for several years” to administer the tax, “paid from the new wealth tax revenues.” Also omitted from the yes line: payment is due in 2027, with an option to spread payments over five years “but would have to pay more to do so,” and residency is fixed as of January 1, 2026.
- The Attorney General also says the revenues are exempt “from constitutional requirements for school funding, budget reserves, and state spending limit.” That exemption is why Props 41 and 42 are described as conflicts. The page’s flow shows the vote-count rule; it does not explain that Prop 40 is written to sit outside the spending limit.
- A campaign estimate of “$100 billion over five years” appears in secondary news coverage and is **not** the LAO figure. The page correctly stays with “tens of billions.” — [Word & Brown / industry news, Sept. 29, 2026](https://jrreport.wordandbrown.com/2026/09/29/billionaire-tax-is-dividing-california-healthcare-system-its-designed-to-support/)

### Gaps
- The statutory phase-in formula and the trust-threshold rule were not quoted from the official text PDF (`https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop40-text-proposed-laws.pdf`). They are reported from law-firm and annotated-text readings.
- Whether the proposed law’s findings section itself cites federal health-care cuts was not confirmed from that PDF. It is absent from the Attorney General summary and the LAO analysis.
- Hoover’s claim of annual spending caps of $22.5 billion and $2.5 billion was not in the LAO analysis or the Attorney General summary and is not treated as established.

## Proposition 41 — special-tax audits and the spending limit

### Takeaway
Audits, “low millions” of dollars a year, “mostly” paid from the new tax, and an unknown net effect match the LAO. The spending-limit rule is a general prohibition on new state taxes that exclude their revenue from the 1979 limit, plus a same-ballot conflict clause. It does not name Prop 40. “Poison pill” is loaded language. Recommendations are not described as binding.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Requires audits for new special taxes and blocks exemptions from the state spending limit.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “Prohibits any new state taxes that exclude their revenues from the state spending limit approved by voters in 1979.” “For statewide special taxes, requires (1) a pre-election audit of programs that would receive funding from a voter-proposed special tax, and (2) recurring audits of programs funded by special taxes enacted after January 1, 2026.” The LAO is softer on the limit: “Under Proposition 41, the state might not be able to exclude new special tax spending from the limit.” The page’s “blocks” tracks the Attorney General’s “Prohibits,” not the LAO’s “might not.” — [Prop 41 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)
- Yes-line: “New special-tax programs face pre-election and four-year audits; revenue stays under the Gann Limit.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE, slightly overbroad.** LAO: the pre-election audit is for special taxes “proposed through the voter initiative process,” after supporters collect 25 percent of signatures, “before it is known whether the initiative will qualify.” Ongoing audits of programs funded by special taxes “created or increased by the Legislature or by voters after January 1, 2026” “would happen every four years.” Legislatively enacted special taxes do not face the pre-election audit. “Revenue stays under the Gann Limit” is broader than “prohibits new state taxes that exclude their revenues” from the limit. The LAO yes statement says the state “might not be able to exclude any new special tax spending from its spending limit.” — [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)
- Cost line: “Net effect unknown; audit costs likely low millions per year, mostly paid from new tax revenue.” **ACCURATE.** LAO: “The net fiscal effect of Proposition 41 is unknown.” “These costs likely would be in the range of the low millions of dollars per year but would grow over time.” “Mostly paid from the new revenues collected from the special taxes.” One-time audits of taxes that never reach the ballot or that voters reject “would be paid from the state’s General Fund.” Voter-guide summaries could add “a few hundred thousand dollars per qualified initiative.” — [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)
- “Adds independent review before and after voters approve special-tax programs.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** The auditor is independent. “Before and after voters approve” fits voter-proposed taxes. It does not fit the pre-election piece for taxes the Legislature enacts; those get the recurring audit only. — [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)
- “Stops initiatives from carving revenue out of the voter-approved spending limit.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE** against the Attorney General, **stronger than the LAO**, and narrower than the rule. The Attorney General prohibition applies to “any new state taxes” that exclude revenue from the limit, not only to initiatives. The LAO says “might not.” — [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/title-summary.htm); [LAO](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)
- “Functions as a poison pill against Prop 40.” **Loaded label, not an LAO finding.** The legal mechanism is general: a ban on spending-limit exclusions, plus “other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes.” The Attorney General summary does not name Proposition 40. Prop 40 is exposed because its own official summary “Exempts such tax revenues from … state spending limit.” LAO: if Prop 41 receives more yes votes, Prop 40 “could be stopped” because “the courts could find” a conflict. The official argument against Prop 41 does say it was written to undo the billionaire tax: “PROP. 41 WOULD CANCEL OUT THE BILLIONAIRE TAX. Prop. 41 is a billionaire-funded scheme designed specifically to undo the California Billionaire Tax.” That is a campaign argument in the voter guide, not the LAO. — [Prop 41 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026); [arguments against](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/41/arguments-rebuttals.htm); [Prop 40 title, exemption bullet](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/40/title-summary.htm)
- “Adds recurring audit bureaucracy without guaranteeing recommendations are adopted.” **ACCURATE** as a description of the LAO, and it is in the con box. LAO: savings are possible “if policymakers implement the State Auditor’s recommendations and those recommendations prove effective. Any resulting savings would depend on future decisions and are not known.” The fiscal summary says “Possible savings associated with implementing recommendations from the audits.” Nothing in the LAO says the recommendations are binding. The pre-election audit must include “identifying ways to reduce an audited program’s annual costs by 10 percent.” That is an identification requirement, not a required 10 percent cut. The page does not claim the 10 percent cut is mandatory; it also does not mention the 10 percent figure. — [LAO Prop 41](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=41&year=2026)

### Inferences
- Calling Prop 41 a poison pill is a fair description of political purpose only if one adopts the opposition’s “designed specifically” claim. As a legal description, it is a general rule that would void Prop 40 only as a consequence of a conflict plus a higher yes-vote total. The page puts the label in the con box, which matches how the guide says arguments are labeled, but the word is not neutral and is not the LAO’s.
- Omitted and scale-relevant: audit costs “would grow over time”; some audits are General Fund costs; printing costs are extra; net effect is unknown in both directions because recommendations might save money.

### Gaps
- The proposed-law PDF was not checked to see whether the constitutional text names “Proposition 40” even though the Attorney General summary does not.

## Proposition 42 — personal-property and retroactive taxes

### Takeaway
The measure blocks new state taxes on owning personal property, including financial assets, and restricts new retroactive state taxes, effective for taxes enacted or taking effect on or after January 1, 2026. The LAO fiscal line is only that future revenue may not rise as much. “Progressive revenue options” is wider than the ban. Nullifying Prop 40 is conditional on a conflict and more yes votes; it is an effect, and the page’s “immediate function” is argument.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Constitutionally blocks new state taxes on owning personal property and most retroactive taxes.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “Prohibits new state taxes on personal property, including business interests, intellectual property, and financial assets (including assets in retirement and investment accounts).” “Prohibits new state taxes that apply retroactively based on the taxpayer’s conduct, activities, or a status that occurred before the new tax’s effective date.” The official bullets do not say “most.” The LAO says the measure “limits the situations” in which a ballot measure or the Legislature can raise any tax retroactively, which supports a limit rather than an absolute ban. A secondary reproduction of the text describes a 365-day exception for certain statutes; that exception was not confirmed from the official PDF in this pass. — [Prop 42 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 42](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026); [Ballotpedia, quoting initiative text](https://ballotpedia.org/California_Proposition_42,_Prohibit_New_Taxes_on_Retirement_Holdings,_Personal_Assets,_and_Savings_and_Limit_Retroactive_Taxes_Initiative_(2026))
- Yes-line: “The state loses those tax options; conflicting taxes enacted after Jan. 1, 2026 are voided.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “Applies to new taxes that are enacted or take effect on or after January 1, 2026.” Separately: “States that other voter-approved measures appearing on the same ballot are void if they conflict with this measure and this measure receives more affirmative votes.” The page folds the date and the conflict clause into one sentence and, in this line, drops the “more affirmative votes” condition. The card’s con line adds that condition for Prop 40. LAO yes statement is narrower and does not mention the date or voiding: “The state could not establish new taxes on the ownership of financial assets or other personal property.” — [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 42](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026)
- Cost line: “Not quantifiable. The LAO says future revenue may not rise as much.” **ACCURATE.** LAO summary: “Possibility that tax revenues will not go up as much in the future.” Narrative: “When and by how much future revenues would be reduced is unclear.” — [LAO Prop 42](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026)
- “Treats retroactive taxation as unfair.” **Argument, not a fact error.** It is in “Best case for.” The method note says those are good-faith cases. The LAO and the Attorney General summary do not find that retroactive taxation is unfair. No correction is required unless a reader treats the pro box as an official finding.
- “Permanently removes progressive revenue options.” **MISLEADING as a description of legal scope; it is a con-box characterization.** The ban is on new taxes on the ownership of personal property (business interests, intellectual property, financial assets, retirement and investment accounts) and on defined retroactive taxes. The LAO yes/no statements do not say income-tax rates, sales taxes, or other graduated taxes are repealed or forbidden. “Progressive revenue options” is wider than that ban. — [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 42](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026)
- “Its immediate function is to nullify Prop 40 if it wins more Yes votes.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE on the condition; “immediate function” is argument.** LAO: if Prop 42 receives more yes votes than Prop 40, Prop 40 “could be stopped from becoming law” because “the courts could find” a conflict. The measure’s own summary states that conflicting same-ballot measures “are void” if this measure has more affirmative votes. The enduring legal change is the constitutional ban, not only the 2026 interaction. The page places the sentence in the con box. — [LAO Prop 42](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=42&year=2026); [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/42/title-summary.htm)

### Inferences
- Existing personal-property taxes are not described as repealed. The LAO background says the vehicle license fee and the local business-equipment property tax already exist, and the proposal is about new taxes. The page’s “new” is the right word.
- Omitted: the retroactivity rule, as summarized, covers taxes keyed to past conduct or status, including — in secondary text — residency on a date before the tax’s effective date. That is the mechanism aimed at Prop 40’s January 1, 2026 residency snapshot. The LAO does not spell out that example.

### Gaps
- The 365-day retroactivity exception was not verified against `https://vig.cdn.sos.ca.gov/2026/general/pdf/prop42-text-proposed-laws.pdf`. Until that PDF is read, “most” should not be treated as a quoted official term.

## Proposition 43 — two-thirds vote for local special taxes

### Takeaway
Citizen-proposed local special taxes are, under the official current-law statement, subject to a majority vote, and this legislatively referred amendment would require two-thirds beginning January 1, 2027. The referral votes were 68–2 in the Assembly and 35–1 in the Senate on ACA 22. The measure, as officially summarized, is about special taxes, not general taxes. The LAO does not say it changes the state General Fund.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Raises the bar for citizen-proposed local special taxes from a majority to two-thirds.” **ACCURATE.** Attorney General: “this two-thirds vote requirement does not currently apply to special taxes put on ballot by voter signature gathering.” “This measure would limit voters’ ability to pass voter-proposed local special taxes by raising the percentage of votes needed to approve such ballot measures from a majority (over 50%) to two-thirds.” LAO: “recent court decisions have allowed special taxes that are proposed by voters to be approved with a majority vote. Under Proposition 43, beginning on January 1, 2027, any new, increased, or extended local special taxes proposed by voters would require a two-thirds vote.” The LAO does not name *California Cannabis Coalition v. City of Upland*. — [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 43](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026)
- Yes-line: “Citizen initiatives face the same two-thirds threshold as government-proposed special taxes.” **ACCURATE** relative to the LAO’s description of current law: government-proposed local special taxes already require two-thirds; voter-proposed ones would too. — [LAO Prop 43](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026)
- No-line: “Citizen-proposed local special taxes may still pass by simple majority.” **ACCURATE** as the official description of a no vote’s current-law result. LAO no statement: “Certain local government taxes could continue to be approved by voters with a majority vote.” — [LAO Prop 43](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026)
- Cost line: “Unknown; local tax revenue may not grow as much. This does not directly affect the state General Fund.” The first two clauses are **ACCURATE.** LAO: “local governments’ tax revenues could be lower in the future than they otherwise would be. The actual revenue impact is unknown.” Summary: “Possibility that local government tax revenues will not go up as much in the future due to a higher vote threshold for certain taxes.” The General Fund sentence is **not an LAO sentence.** The LAO fiscal estimate mentions only local revenue. No state General Fund figure is given. **MOSTLY ACCURATE** as an inference. — [LAO Prop 43](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026)
- “despite bipartisan legislative referral.” The referral is **confirmed.** Voter guide: “FINAL VOTES CAST BY THE LEGISLATURE ON ACA 22 (PROPOSITION 43) (CHAPTER 132, STATUTES OF 2026). Senate: Ayes 35 Noes 1. Assembly: Ayes 68 Noes 2.” The LAO title calls it a “Legislative Constitutional Amendment.” “Bipartisan” is **not printed** on that table. The aye totals are large enough that the vote was not a one-vote majority, but party labels were not retrieved from the official table. — [title summary, vote table](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/title-summary.htm)
- “Applies one threshold regardless of who proposes the special tax.” **ACCURATE** for special taxes. The official materials reviewed do not extend the change to general taxes. LAO background lists “cities, counties, special districts, and school districts” as local governments. The operative sentence is “any new, increased, or extended local special taxes proposed by voters.” No sentence found in the LAO analysis or the Attorney General summary excludes school parcel taxes or limits coverage to cities and counties. — [LAO Prop 43](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=43&year=2026); [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/43/title-summary.htm)

### Inferences
- Secondary guides disagree about an extra property-tax ban. The Sacramento Bee and Ballotpedia describe Prop 43 as also prohibiting new local ad valorem property taxes except bond debt-service taxes. SPUR says the legislative compromise “sidelines some of the more complex issues” in a withdrawn Howard Jarvis initiative and is “focused only on the voter threshold.” The Attorney General summary and the LAO analysis, which control here, describe only the two-thirds rule. The property-tax ban is not treated as part of Prop 43. — [SPUR](https://www.spur.org/voter-guide/2026-11/ca-prop-43-local-tax-threshold); [Ballotpedia](https://ballotpedia.org/California_Proposition_43,_Two-Thirds_Vote_Requirement_for_Local_Special_Tax_Initiatives_and_Property_Tax_Initiative_Prohibition_Amendment_(2026)); [Sacramento Bee, Sept. 17, 2026](https://www.sacbee.com/news/politics-government/election/article316990490.html)
- Omitted official detail: the new threshold applies to voter-proposed measures approved after January 1, 2027, and it covers new, increased, or extended special taxes, not only brand-new ones.
- *Upland* (2017) is the case commentators cite for the citizen-initiative path. The LAO’s formulation — “recent court decisions” allowing a majority for voter-proposed special taxes — is the official current-law statement used for this verdict. Later appellate cases, not a single sentence in *Upland*, are what commentators often mean by the two-thirds holding. That distinction does not change the page’s description of current law, which matches the Attorney General.

### Gaps
- Party-by-party vote counts for ACA 22 were not pulled from leginfo. “Bipartisan” is an inference from 68–2 and 35–1, not a label in the voter-guide table.
- No sentence was found that either expressly includes or expressly excludes school-district parcel taxes beyond the LAO’s general “local special taxes” and its list of local governments that includes school districts.

## Proposition 44 — 90 percent clinic spending rule

### Takeaway
The 90 percent rule, the shortfall penalty, the five-year refund, waivers, and “low tens of millions” in fee-covered enforcement costs match the official summaries. The page’s cost line omits other uncertain state and local costs the LAO flags. “Provider groups warn…” is an attributed argument, not an LAO finding, though the LAO itself says some clinics might close.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Requires nonprofit safety-net clinics to spend 90% of revenue on program services.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** The official title uses “program services.” Attorney General: penalties on “nonprofit Federally Qualified Health Centers (community clinics providing primary care to medically underserved areas and populations) that spend less than 90% of revenue on ‘program services’ advancing their charitable purpose, including but not limited to patient services.” LAO: “private nonprofit safety net clinics” must “spend at least 90 percent of their total revenue each year on providing health care services,” so other spending is “no more than 10 percent.” Public clinics are outside the rule. The page’s “nonprofit safety-net” follows the LAO and the measure title more than the Attorney General’s “Federally Qualified Health Centers” label. No revenue-dollar threshold appears in either official summary. — [Prop 44 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026)
- Yes-line: “Covered clinics must meet the 90% standard or pay the shortfall as a penalty.” **ACCURATE**, and incomplete. LAO: “The penalty would equal the amount of spending needed to reach the 90 percent minimum.” Attorney General: “Monetary penalties may be refunded if centers become compliant within five years.” LAO: if they do not comply within five years, “the state would keep the money and spend it on clinic workforce programs.” — [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026); [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/title-summary.htm)
- Cost line: “Low tens of millions a year for enforcement, covered by fees on affected clinics.” **ACCURATE** for the enforcement cost the LAO quantified. “State enforcement costs would be in the low tens of millions of dollars per year. The proposition directs the state to cover the costs by charging fees on affected clinics.” **Omitted:** “Proposition 44 could create other costs for the state and local governments. These costs are uncertain.” Examples: higher Medi-Cal costs if clinics spend more on covered services, and “other uncertain effects” if clinics close. — [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026)
- Flow: at least 90 percent program services, at most 10 percent other spending; fees fund enforcement; penalty equals the shortfall. **ACCURATE** against the LAO’s 90/10 and penalty sentences. The LAO says the Attorney General defines which expenses count, “using existing reports to the federal government as a starting point,” so “program services” versus administration is not fully specified in the measure and is not identical, in the LAO’s paraphrase, to a frozen federal Form 990 category. — [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026)
- “five-year cure period” in the pro argument. **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** The official mechanism is a refund of penalties if the clinic becomes compliant within five years, not a statement that no penalty is owed during a five-year grace period. Waivers are real: Attorney General, the Department of Public Health “may waive spending requirements in exceptional circumstances.” LAO: clinics “could ask the state for a temporary waiver of the requirements in some cases.” — [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/44/title-summary.htm); [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026)
- “Provider groups warn clinics could reduce services or close.” **Attributed argument, not presented as an LAO finding.** It is in “Best case against.” Separately, the LAO says, as an uncertain fiscal effect, “Some clinics that could not meet the minimum spending requirement might close instead.” The page does not cite the LAO for that sentence. Current reported spending level, omitted from the page: LAO says these clinics “currently report spending an average of about 80 percent of their revenue on providing health care services.” — [LAO Prop 44](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=44&year=2026)

### Inferences
- The cost line the user flagged is the part that matches the LAO. The omitted uncertain Medi-Cal and closure costs are the scale caveat: “low tens of millions” is enforcement only.
- Also omitted from the official title: criminal charges “for false reports or artificially increasing spending ratio.” That is a mechanism, not a fiscal estimate.

### Gaps
- Whether FQHC look-alikes are covered, and whether any revenue floor exists in the proposed law, was not confirmed from the text PDF. Neither appears in the LAO analysis or the Attorney General summary.

## Proposition 45 — CEQA deadlines and narrowed review

### Takeaway
The card’s cost sentence matches the LAO: initial state and local costs likely in the high tens of millions a year, potentially over $100 million, partly fee-funded, with unknown longer-term effects. The bar’s $80 million to $110 million band does not. The measure does not only speed review; the Attorney General says it limits alternatives, evidence, and judicial relief. No fee share is published.

### Cited Findings
- One-line: “Sets deadlines and narrows lawsuits for eligible housing, infrastructure, energy, and health projects.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE, incomplete.** Attorney General: expedited review for “specified project categories (including most housing, transportation, water, health, wildfire mitigation, educational facility, internet access, and clean energy projects).” The page’s “infrastructure” and “energy” roughly cover transportation, water, and clean energy, and it omits wildfire mitigation, educational facilities, and internet access. “Narrows lawsuits” matches “limits evidence courts may consider and relief courts can order” and “deadlines for filing and resolving lawsuits.” — [Prop 45 title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/title-summary.htm)
- Yes-line: “Eligible projects may opt into faster CEQA review and court procedures.” Opt-in is **ACCURATE.** LAO: “The proposition gives eligible project applicants the option of using the new procedures or existing procedures.” “Faster” is **incomplete.** The same sentence of the LAO says the measure “(1) tightens time limits … (2) changes parts of the CEQA review process; and (3) changes the court’s review process.” The Attorney General says it allows “expedited review of environmental impacts, limiting public agencies’ current obligation to consider a range of feasible project alternatives to reduce environmental impacts.” — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026); [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/title-summary.htm)
- Cost line: “High tens of millions, potentially over $100M a year, partly fee-funded; long-term effects unknown.” **ACCURATE.** LAO: “These costs likely would be in the high tens of millions of dollars annually, potentially exceeding $100 million annually.” “Fees paid by project applicants and people filing lawsuits would cover some of these costs.” “After this initial period, while some costs could continue, others likely would decline.” Longer term: “uncertain but potentially more significant” positive or negative effects. Ballot label: “partly covered by fees.” — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026)
- Bar: `{prop:45, low:.08, high:.11, label:'High tens of millions; possibly over $100M / year'}`. **MISLEADING precision.** 0.08 is $80 million and 0.11 is $110 million on the page’s billion-dollar scale. The LAO does not state an $80 million floor or a $110 million ceiling. “Potentially exceeding $100 million” is an open upper tail. A bar that stops at $110 million presents a cap the LAO did not give. The label’s words are closer to the LAO than the bar’s width. These are also “initial” costs “for the state and local governments, including state courts,” “partly” offset by fees — not a permanent General Fund cost of $80–110 million. The section title “Largest yearly General Fund effects” overstates what the LAO assigned to the General Fund. — `index.html`; [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026)
- “partly fee-funded” — **ACCURATE, and no share is published.** LAO: fees “would cover some of these costs” and “would partly cover these costs.” The page’s own flow note says “exact state/fee shares are not published.” That note is correct. — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026)
- “Could get housing and infrastructure built faster while keeping environmental standards.” **Argument.** The speed clause is a possible outcome, not a guarantee. “Keeping environmental standards” is **not** what the official summary says. The Attorney General says the measure limits the duty to consider a range of feasible alternatives “to reduce environmental impacts.” LAO changes include: impacts assessed under “the laws and standards in place at the time the project application is submitted,” whereas current law lets the agency “consider new information and revise its review standards”; the applicant may develop “only one project alternative”; consultation “only with California native tribes that have been formally recognized by the federal government,” versus “a wider range of tribes”; public comment is limited in duration, whereas current law “sets a minimum number of days—not a maximum.” Longer-term, the LAO says changes could lead to “approval of projects with negative environmental impacts that would not have otherwise occurred.” The pro sentence is not an LAO finding, and its standards clause is in tension with the LAO. — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026); [title](https://voterguide.sos.ca.gov/propositions/45/title-summary.htm)
- “Limits project-wide shutdowns when only one portion is out of compliance.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE.** LAO: the proposition “prevents the court from stopping the whole project if it finds that the agency did not follow CEQA correctly. Instead, the court can only stop the part of the project where the environmental review did not comply with CEQA while the agency fixes the problem.” The official defect is a CEQA-review failure on part of the project, not a physical “portion … out of compliance” in a more general sense. — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026)
- “Caps public input and narrows judicial and tribal review.” **MOSTLY ACCURATE**, with tighter official wording. Public input: the LAO describes a cap on how long comments are accepted, not a cap on the number of comments. Judicial: “reduces the scope of what the court can consider in CEQA lawsuits” and limits the remedy, as above. Tribal: consultation is narrowed to federally recognized California tribes, not described as a thinner review for tribes that remain covered. The con box is where this sentence sits; the underlying limits are LAO findings, not only campaign rhetoric. — [LAO Prop 45](https://lao.ca.gov/BallotAnalysis/Proposition?number=45&year=2026)

### Inferences
- Displayed GF percentages for the bar, using the same `pct` function, would be about 0.03 percent to 0.04 percent of the $251.477 billion spending total (`0.08/251.477 ≈ 0.0318 percent`; `0.11/251.477 ≈ 0.0437 percent`). Those percentages are also absent from the LAO. They are not printed in the Prop 45 cost sentence; they appear only if the impact row renders.
- Omitted categories and reported exclusions change who can opt in. Secondary guides list exclusions including nuclear plants, new oil and gas facilities, high-speed rail, Delta conveyance, and jails or detention facilities. Those exclusions were not in the LAO narrative text retrieved here (the LAO points to an unread Figure 1) and are not in the Attorney General bullets, which use “including.” They are not used as official verdicts. — [LA Times, Oct. 1, 2026](https://www.latimes.com/california/story/2026-10-01/2026-california-election-proposition-45-ceqa-housing-data-center-voter-guide); [SPUR](https://www.spur.org/voter-guide/2026-11/ca-prop-45-environmental-review-ceqa)
- Example deadline that is in the LAO and not on the page: an applicant may seek a hearing, and potentially sue, if an agency misses a “365-business-day time limit for finishing the EIR.”

### Gaps
- Figure 1 on the LAO page (`/ballot/2026/prop45-fig1.png`) was not transcribed, so the official eligible-project list beyond the Attorney General’s “including” clause is incomplete here.

### Takeaway
