RESEARCH RECORD · OCTOBER 5, 2026

What the ballot guide rests on

A check of the California 2026 ballot explainer against the official voter-guide analyses, the enacted budget, and the pollsters’ own releases. It does not say how to vote. The guide’s ballot text was not changed. The guide links here from “How this guide was built.”

Election Tuesday, November 3, 2026 Page checked ca-2026-ballot/index.html Full chain grok_research.md

How to read this. The sentence under each title is the conclusion in ordinary language. Open “Show the evidence” for the quotation, the arithmetic, the grade, and the strongest fair defense of the original wording. A document mismatch is labeled a fact. A choice about emphasis, color, or how to draw a chart is labeled a judgment.

THE STANDARD

Fact, judgment, and grade

Two people who open the same document should get the same sentence. When they do, and the explainer says something else, that mismatch is a fact. When the documents support more than one reasonable sentence, the choice between them is a judgment. A judgment can be well supported. It is still not a measurement.

Fact

Settled by a document or a repeated calculation

Example: the poll release prints 44 percent no, and the card says that share was not published.

Judgment

A reading, a weight, or a chart choice

Example: whether one party color can stand for a coalition that is split. The endorsements are facts. The color is a judgment.

Grades A, B, C

How close the quotation is

A opened and quoted here. B recorded from the primary document in this review, and not re-opened while the research file was written. C arithmetic from A or B inputs.

How the check was done

Every sentence and number a reader can see was treated as a claim: the hero, the budget charts, the fourteen cards, the yes and no lines, the flow diagrams, the pro and con bullets, the poll figures, the coalition labels, the color assigned in code, the glossary, and the source links.

Pro and con boxes are labeled as each side’s case. A bullet there was graded as a false finding only when it stated a document fact the cited document does not support. A value judgment inside a labeled argument box was left as argument. Titles, one-liners, cost lines, type tags, chart labels, colors, and the overview were held to the standard of a neutral description.

Source order

A lower source was not allowed to override a higher one.

  1. The statute or constitutional text, and the official title and summary prepared for the ballot.
  2. The Legislative Analyst’s Office ballot analysis, and the LAO overview of the enacted 2026–27 budget, Report 5197, August 26, 2026.
  3. Department of Finance enacted schedules and the public budget overview. These are the administration’s estimates through July 1, 2026. Report 5197 uses those estimates and says so.
  4. The pollster’s own release: question wording, population, dates, sample size, and topline.
  5. Official argument signers in the voter guide, and published endorsement lists.
  6. News and advocacy pages, only to identify who said something, and labeled as secondary.

The June 25, 2026 Secretary of State qualification press release prints an earlier fiscal estimate for several initiatives. The voter-guide analysis is later. Where they differ, the voter-guide analysis is the estimate a ballot explainer should use. The page does that on the items checked.

Arithmetic

Page figures stored in billions were converted to dollars by multiplying by 1,000,000,000. LAO table figures are in millions of dollars. Schedule 8 and Schedule 9 figures are in thousands of dollars. The unit is named at each step.

Percentages use the page’s own expenditure total unless a step says otherwise. The page function is pct(value) = (value / totalSpending * 100).toFixed(2). toFixed(2) rounds to two decimal places. That rounding is part of the Proposition 39 finding.

PROPOSITION 40 · TAX BASE

The 5 percent applies to net worth

The Legislative Analyst says billionaires who were California residents on January 1, 2026 would pay a one-time tax equal to 5 percent of their net worth. Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts generally would be excluded. The explainer’s one-liner says “covered wealth above $1B at up to 5%.”

Other reading. “Above $1B” can identify who is taxed, and “up to 5%” can be the rate on covered wealth. That defense still leaves the exclusions out, and it has to fight the ordinary reading of a tax “above $1 billion at 5 percent,” which is a tax on the excess.

Show the evidence

What the explainer says · grade A

Taxes covered wealth above $1B at up to 5%, mainly for health care.

One-line summary on the explainer.

California collects a one-time 5% wealth tax from covered billionaires and trusts.

Yes box. Closer to the official yes statement. It adds trusts, which the Attorney General title includes and the LAO yes statement does not.

What the Analyst says · grade A

Under Proposition 40, billionaires who were residents of California on January 1, 2026 would have to pay a one-time state tax equal to 5 percent of their net worth. The tax would be due in 2027. Taxpayers would have the option to spread the payments over five years but would have to pay more to do so. Real estate, pensions, and retirement accounts generally would be excluded from the tax.

LAO analysis, proposal section. lao.ca.gov, Proposition 40, 2026.

The state would collect a one-time tax from billionaires equal to 5 percent of their wealth.

Official yes statement on the same LAO page.

What the Attorney General title said at qualification · grade A

Imposes one-time tax of up to 5% on taxpayers and trusts with covered assets valued over $1 billion; covered assets include businesses, securities, art, collectibles, and intellectual property, but exclude real property and some pensions and retirement accounts.

Secretary of State certification press release, June 25, 2026. “Up to 5%” is the official sign that the title does not describe the rate as flat. The LAO narrative says “5 percent of their net worth” and does not print a phase-in table.

Why the two English readings are different amounts · grade C

This illustration is not an official tax bill. It shows the gap between the two readings of the one-liner.

Reading A, whole net worth:  0.05 × $2,000,000,000 = $100,000,000
Reading B, only the excess:  0.05 × ($2,000,000,000 − $1,000,000,000) = $50,000,000
Difference:                   $50,000,000

Near $1.1 billion the two readings differ by much more than half, because the excess is small and the whole fortune is not. A fortune held as real estate is largely outside the tax under both official summaries. The one-liner does not say that.

Fact

The LAO sentence is “5 percent of their net worth.” The Attorney General title is “up to 5%” on taxpayers and trusts with covered assets over $1 billion, with stated exclusions. The one-liner says “covered wealth above $1B at up to 5%.” The illustration’s two products are $100 million and $50 million.

Judgment

Whether every reader must take “above $1B” to mean “only the dollars above $1 billion.” The fair defense is the “who is taxed” reading above. The correction that does not depend on that dispute is to use the LAO’s own words: 5 percent of net worth, with real estate and some retirement accounts excluded.

Not established in this record

The exact phase-in between $1 billion and $1.1 billion. Secondary descriptions of the proposed law give that schedule. The proposed-law PDF was not re-read for the research file. “Up to 5%” remains the official sign in the Attorney General title. The LAO analysis voters are pointed to says 5 percent of net worth and does not print the phase-in.

PROPOSITIONS 40, 41, AND 42 · CONFLICT

The rule is real. The block is not yet automatic.

If two measures on the same ballot both pass and their provisions conflict, the one with the higher number of yes votes prevails. A measure that fails does not enter that comparison. The explainer is right about that direction. It states that 41 or 42 voids 40. The Analyst says a court could find the conflict, and that 40 could then be stopped.

Other reading. Propositions 41 and 42 each tell the reader, in their own official summaries, that a conflicting measure on the same ballot is void if this one receives more affirmative votes. A reader can treat that clause as mandatory once a conflict and a higher yes total exist. A reader who follows the Analyst can say the conflict itself is what a court has to find. Both sentences should be available. The overview currently gives only the certain version.

Show the evidence

What the explainer says · grade A

Prop 40 creates the billionaire tax. Props 41 and 42 can block it. If conflicting measures pass, the one with more Yes votes controls.

Overview. The flow says that if 41 or 42 has more yes votes, Proposition 40 revenue is $0 and the tax is void.

The constitution · grade A

If provisions of two or more measures approved at the same election conflict, the provisions of the measure receiving the highest number of affirmative votes shall prevail.

California Constitution, Article II, section 10(b), as displayed by the Legislature’s code site. Highest yes count, not the higher percentage, and not whichever measure was chaptered later. Only measures that were approved are compared.

What the Analyst says · grade A

If Proposition 41 or 42 on this same ballot receive more “yes” votes than Proposition 40, then Proposition 40 could be stopped from becoming law even if it gets yes votes from a majority of voters. This is because the courts could find that Proposition 41 or 42 conflict with Proposition 40.

Same LAO Proposition 40 analysis. The word “could” appears twice.

What 41 and 42 say about themselves · grade B

The Attorney General summaries, recorded from the voter guide in this review and not re-opened while the research file was written, each say that another measure on the same ballot is void if it conflicts and if this measure receives more affirmative votes. Those sentences still depend on a conflict existing.

Fact

Section 10(b) uses the highest number of affirmative votes. The LAO says “could be stopped” and “the courts could find.” The explainer states the block as automatic.

Judgment

How certain to sound before a court has ruled. The measures’ text and the Analyst’s “could” answer different questions. Printing only one of them is the presentation choice.

The one-point remainder · grade A for the code, grade C for the subtraction

When both shares are numbers, the page computes Math.max(0, 100 - yes - no). PPIC says numbers may not add to 100 because of rounding. PROPOSITION 1 · THE VETERANS PIECE

Both pieces are general-obligation bonds

Proposition 1 allows $11.25 billion of general-obligation bonds. The General Fund repays the $10 billion housing piece. Veterans’ home-loan payments have covered the $1.25 billion piece, so that piece has operated at no direct cost to the state. The flow chart still labels it “CalVet revenue bond.”

Other reading. The practical repayment is real, and another line on the page already says the loans are borrower-repaid. A loose label can look harmless for that reason. The legal form is still a general-obligation bond. The page already has a revenue-bond measure, Proposition 37, so the same words for both teach the wrong distinction.

Show the evidence

What the explainer says · grade A

The flow contains “CalVet revenue bond” with the subtitle “$1.25B.” A separate card says “Prop 1 · $11.25B · General-obligation bond” and “General Fund repays most debt service.” The cost line says “$500–600M a year for about 25 years” and “CalVet loans are borrower-repaid.”

What the Analyst says · grade A

Proposition 1 allows the state to sell $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds for housing purposes. This amount consists of (1) a $10 billion bond for a variety of state housing programs that would be repaid by the General Fund and (2) a $1.25 billion bond that would be repaid by veterans’ payments on their home loans.

LAO, Proposition 1, 2026.

The fiscal section says the General Fund cost of the $10 billion housing bond would be “$500 million to $600 million annually for about 25 years,” about 0.25 percent of the General Fund budget. On the veterans piece, payments “have always covered the amount owed on the bonds, meaning the program has always operated at no direct cost to the state.” The background section says the existing veterans program “sells general obligation bonds.”

The pledge language · grade B

The proposed-law review recorded Military and Veterans Code section 998.754(a): the veterans bonds “shall be and constitute a valid and legally binding general obligation of the State of California, and the full faith and credit of the State of California is hereby pledged.” That PDF was not re-opened for the research file. The LAO sentences are enough to reject the label “revenue bond.” The statute citation is the additional pledge language.

The contrast with Proposition 37 · grade B

Proposition 37’s LAO analysis, recorded in this review, authorizes revenue bonds, with a statutory legend that the bond is payable from program funds and is not a debt or a pledge of the state’s faith and credit. The page calls Proposition 37 a revenue-bond authority. That label matches. The same words on the Proposition 1 veterans piece do not.

Fact

The LAO calls the $11.25 billion general-obligation bonds and splits repayment $10 billion / $1.25 billion. The page’s flow says “CalVet revenue bond.” This is the only failure in the local fiscal checker.

Judgment

Whether the label is harmless because borrowers repay it in practice. The repayment history is a fact. The instrument’s name is a different fact. The big-number card, which already says general-obligation, is the label that matches the Analyst.

PROPOSITIONS 1, 3, AND 38 · PERCENT OF THE GENERAL FUND

The dollars are the Analyst’s. The percentages are the page’s division.

Propositions 1 and 38 cost $500 million to $600 million a year on the Analyst’s page. The explainer’s “about 0.20–0.24%” is those dollars divided by the $251.477 billion General Fund. Proposition 3’s “about 2–6%” is $5 billion to $15 billion divided by the same baseline. The Analyst prints “about 0.25 percent” for the two bonds and prints no percentage for Proposition 3.

Other reading. The quotients are close to the Analyst’s round “0.25 percent,” and the page’s method note says it is making this comparison. They are a defect only if a reader thinks the Analyst published 0.20–0.24. The underlying cost is the same. Labeling them as the page’s comparison removes the ambiguity.

The June 25 qualification press release said the immunology bond would cost “about $500 million annually for 25 years.” The voter-guide analysis opened for this check says $500–$600 million for about 20 years. The quotients above. The LAO phrases “0.25 percent,” “$5 billion and $15 billion,” and “top 2 percent of California taxpayers.” The 60 percent that is not for schools is for other state programs. The LAO does not dedicate it to health care.

Judgment

Whether a more precise quotient beside a round “0.25 percent” needs a correction. The risk is attribution, not the dollars.

PROPOSITIONS 39 AND 45 · ANNUAL-IMPACT BARS

The words track the Analyst. The bar ends do not.

Proposition 39’s bar runs from $20 million to $300 million and the card says roughly 0.01–0.12 percent of the General Fund. DEFICITS AND RESERVES

Three different billions, each attached to a real definition

Spending minus this year’s revenues and transfers is $24.723 billion. That is the same amount the Special Fund for Economic Uncertainties is drawn down, from $29.223 billion to $4.500 billion. The operating deficit is smaller because a $6.388 billion deposit in a temporary holding account is added back. That account is not a reserve. The administration’s operating-deficit cell is −$18.335 billion. The Analyst’s prose says $18.5 billion. The page copies the Analyst’s sentence and correctly says this is not the same thing as the $24.7 billion gap.

Other reading. $18.5 billion is a real sentence, and attributing it to the Analyst is the right attribution. It is also $165 million above the administration’s cell, and the nearest tenth of $18.335 billion is $18.3 billion. Keeping $18.5 billion alone forces a rounding choice. Showing $18.5 billion as the Analyst’s rounding of −$18.335 billion does not. “About $20 billion” of broadly usable reserves matches the Budget Stabilization Account plus the uncertainty reserve, $19.570 billion. It does not match that pair plus the $9.2 billion school reserve.

SHORT LINES THAT DROP A CONDITION

A one-line summary left out who is covered, or when a rule starts

These are smaller than the six items above. Each omitted or extra condition is a sentence in an official summary. A one-line summary cannot carry every condition. The ones here change who is covered, what voids a ballot, or when a rule starts.

Other reading. Condensing is the point of an explainer. The fair test is whether a reader who relied only on the short line would be wrong about coverage, a voided ballot, or a start date. If yes, the condition belongs in the yes/no line or the sentence under it. Leaving it out is an editorial cut a fact-checker can name. It is not evidence that the rest of the card was invented.

The color is a judgment. The signer list is the fact.

REPEAT THE CHECK

Five steps, in order

  1. Open the explainer and search for the sentence named in the finding.
  2. Open the URL in that finding and search for the quotation.
  3. Repeat the arithmetic in the same units. Billions on the page, millions in the LAO tables, thousands in Schedules 8 and 9.
  4. Decide whether the remaining step is a fact about those two texts or a judgment about emphasis.
  5. Run python3 tools/verify_fiscal/verify_fiscal.py for the budget lines, the bond label, and the chart endpoints. The pins are in tools/verify_fiscal/official_fiscal_2026.json. The script does not use the network.

A correction that cannot survive steps 2 and 3 should be dropped. A judgment that is labeled as a fact should be relabeled, even when the judgment is reasonable.
